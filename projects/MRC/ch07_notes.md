> We first drew this figure with Eugene, Oregon, and its strip told a striking story: from 1970 to 1999 only one year in ten fell outside
> temperate seasonal forest, but since 2000 more than two years in three have. It looked like a forest slipping toward shrubland. It was
> a rain gauge. Set beside two neighbours with long records, Salem and Corvallis, Eugene's rainfall crept upward from the 1950s until it
> was running 40 percent above theirs, then dropped abruptly in 1995 -- the year the airport's staffed weather office closed and an automated station took over -- and has
> stayed slightly below them since. Neighbouring
> stations share their weather; they do not share their instruments. A step at one station that its neighbours do not see points to a
> change in how the measurement was made, not in what was measured. The category changed because the operation changed. Long-term
> monitoring is difficult, and the conditions of measurement do change -- a gauge is replaced, a station moves, a tree grows up beside
> it -- and every such change can move a site across a boundary that the vegetation never crossed.

**Numbers behind it (GHCN-Daily):** period means Eugene 107 / 131 / 91 cm (1940-69 / 1970-99 / 2000-25); Salem 104 / 103 / 98;
Corvallis 99 / 113 / 106. Eugene/neighbour ratio 0.98 (1950-54) rising to 1.42 (1990-94), 1.00 (1995-99), ~0.9 since. Instrument change: NWS staffed
operations ended in August 1995; the automated station (ASOS) became fully operational on 1 September 1995 -- the year Eugene's ratio to its
neighbours steps down (Kim's research; NWS Climate Book Eugene). The station's history: University of Oregon co-op from 1890, several town
sites, the airpark (1928, first-order 1937), Mahlon Sweet Field from December 1942, the old control tower 1987-1995, ASOS since. The earlier
upward drift (1950s-1994) is not explained by any documented move -- an honest loose end worth saying so in the text.
