## The access story — why digital records are "more than a convenience"
*(Kim, 2026-07-08. Candidate home: Chapter 1, "The records" — as the human reason
a global digital aggregator matters. See GBIF-definition note below.)*

Before specimen data were transferred to databases — and then, years later, many
of those databases consolidated into GBIF — seeing a specimen meant one of two
things: you wrote to a herbarium and requested a **loan**, and the sheet was
shipped to you by **mail**; or you **travelled to the herbarium yourself**.

Kim once needed to make an in-person visit to the **Central National Herbarium**
near **Kolkata, India**, to see specimens he required. That day, what sounds like
a simple scholarly errand turned out to be **genuinely dangerous — life
threatening**. (The reasons are not important to the book; the point is that a
"simple task" was nothing of the kind.)

During **COVID-19**, Kim tried to get access to several of the large **US
herbaria**. They were **off limits**. Again: something seemingly simple turned out
to be the opposite.

**The point:** for Kim, online digital records are *more than a convenience*. The
distance between "I need to see that specimen" and actually seeing it has, at
times, been measured in oceans, mail delays, closed doors — and, once, in
physical danger. Digital aggregation collapses that distance to a query.

Register note: tell it plainly and briefly, first person, without melodrama —
the understatement ("the reasons are not important here") is the power of it.

## Idea to seed the frontier / invitation chapter (Kim, 2026-07-08)
The collecting-mode diagnostic (Ch. 4) is interesting *because it varies*. Kim's
extension: **ethnobotanical collections** would likely look ecological, not
taxonomic — because collection is tied to *use* and *place*, so a species may be
gathered repeatedly wherever it is used, rather than once per region. So the same
Chao1 "completeness" signal that reads as a collecting-mode fingerprint at Kīpuka
would read differently for an ethnobotanical corpus. Candidate home: the
Limits/Frontier chapter, as part of "point this at your own place and see what it
says" — the tool as a way to *characterize how a corpus was assembled*, across
disciplines. A genuinely new use the alert reader can try.

## The big idea — aggregation as a knowledge engine (Kim, 2026-07-09)
*(Cross-cutting thesis. Capstone of Ch.7 Status; echo in Ch.10 Limits/Frontier; may
strengthen the Preface thesis. Verbatim sense preserved.)*

Consolidating herbaria and creating very inclusive databases (GIFT/GBIF) produces
**resources that were not available to a traditional herbarium.** Our original focus was
getting a checklist, but the analysis process — drawing on these new resources — can now
**go beyond the list of species and help sharpen our knowledge of the species.** A
highlighted area of conflict is **where an interesting problem lives.** In turn, the
aggregated database **gains increased value**, and in the process **should garner
increased support.** Specialty herbaria, such as the **National Tropical Botanical
Garden**, should see that adding their collection to GBIF is **more than a matter of
providing an alternative means of access — it is a step in learning more about their
collection and other collections.**

Why it matters for the book: it turns the whole enterprise from *retrieval* into
*discovery*. Ch.1 sells aggregation as **access**; this sells it as **knowledge
production**. Concrete hook already in the book: NTBG's Kauaʻi collections (Ken Wood) are
what make the ʻIliau checklist (Ch.9) possible — so the institutional argument is not
hypothetical, it is running in our own second site.

## Placement plan for the GBIF passage
Plan for that passage:
1. One compact definition — GBIF = the **Global Biodiversity Information
   Facility**, an international open-data aggregator that pools digitized
   specimen and observation records from thousands of institutions (herbaria,
   museums, surveys) into one queryable index; we use its **preserved-specimen**
   (herbarium) records.
2. Immediately follow with the **access story** above as the *why it matters* —
   the aggregation is not mere tidiness; it is what makes the specimens reachable
   at all.
