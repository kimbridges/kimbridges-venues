# My Name Is Nike
*A short story*

---

## Chapter 1 — The Name on My Forehead

The walls of the studio apartment were papered with photographs of herself: Rebecca in a dugout canoe, Rebecca grinning under a thatch roof, Rebecca sunburned and radiant, holding up a fistful of leaves as though she had invented them. She had been Becky for twenty-six years and Nike for eleven weeks, and she still answered to both, which made introductions long.

She propped her phone against a coffee mug, took a breath she felt was full of purpose, and pressed record.

"Hello, Dr. McClatchey. My name is Rebecca. You can call me Becky." A pause. "Most people call me — well, they used to call me Becky. Now they call me Nike. Perhaps I should explain."

She had gone to South America, she told him, to learn about drugs. She said it the way other people said they had gone to find themselves, and in fairness she had found something. A guide — she'd met him online, he had excellent reviews — had offered to take her into a remote village for a ceremony. A special event. She should participate. She had participated.

"Two days later, when I woke up, I felt pretty groggy," she said, and even now, recording, she smiled at the memory, because everyone in the village had been smiling too. They had smiled and pointed at her forehead, and she had smiled back, assuming this was how you were welcomed.

It was at the hotel, in the foxed mirror over the sink, that she had read herself. NIKE, in blunt blue capitals, just below the hairline. Backwards in the glass, then forwards when her mind caught up.

She had called the guide. Why would they do that?

That was the ceremony, he'd said, puzzled she hadn't understood. They put your name on you. You passed out — the drink — so they took the name from your shirt. The pocket. He reminded her, kindly, of the missionary boys who had come through once with their names printed on little tags above the heart, and how the village had admired such a sensible custom: a people who labeled themselves so a stranger always knew who he was talking to.

"That's the brand," she had told him. "That's not my name. That's the company that made the shirt."

He had been unmoved. And when she said she would have it removed, he had grown almost frightened on her behalf. Very bad luck, he'd said. It would bring her more distress than she could imagine. She must leave it as it was.

She left it as it was. She also, on returning to the States, retained a lawyer and made it true. If she was going to wear the word, she reasoned, it might as well be hers. Nike wasn't a bad name. It had a ring.

"This experience," she told McClatchey's voicemail, warming now, "makes me want to do graduate work in your program. I've submitted my application." She hesitated, then decided authenticity required it. "Please don't look at my grades. I don't think they reflect my true abilities — and anyway, I can do fieldwork in remote villages. I've proven that."

There was one more thing, the real thing, the thing she had been saving.

While she was in the village she had noticed that everyone was extraordinarily healthy. She had asked about it, and they had told her: there was a plant. A special plant that kept them well. She had not seen it clearly, had not learned its name, had not asked who tended it or what it cost them — but she knew, with the certainty of someone who has slept through the only hard part, that the world needed it.

"I've just got to go back and get this plant," she said, "and bring it to the civilized world. I'm sure the villagers won't mind giving it to me." She paused on the threshold of the sentence, then walked through it, because it seemed the strongest argument she had. "After all — I've got my name on my forehead."

She ended the recording, listened to it once, and found it moving. Then she sent it to a man who had spent twenty-five years learning, among other things, that the surest sign of trouble is a stranger certain the village won't mind.

## Chapter 2 — From Tattoo to PhD Program

Will McClatchey had spent twenty-five years in ethnobotany and believed himself past surprise. The recording cured him of the belief in just under four minutes.

He played it a second time so Andrea Liu could hear it. She had come in to borrow a stapler and stayed, leaning in the doorframe while the afternoon fell through the jalousie windows in slats and lay across the herbarium cabinets. When the message ended — *I'm sure the villagers won't mind giving it to me* — she let the silence run a beat.

"She's lucky," Liu said, "that 'Nike' is only four letters." She was a medical anthropologist and listened to the recording the way a physician hears a cough across a waiting room. "That whole story is a failure of informed consent wearing a souvenir. The tattoo. Whatever they gave her to drink. And now the plant — she's worked out how to take it before it's occurred to her to ask whether it's hers to take."

"I heard that part," McClatchey said.

"So you'll reject her."

"I don't think I will."

Liu raised an eyebrow, which on her was an entire argument.

"Listen to it again," he said, "but underneath. Strip off the bravado and there's something I can't reject — she's *curious*, and curiosity is the only raw material I can't teach. If I send her a rejection letter, here is what she learns: that science looked at her and shut the door. She'll be a martyr to her own legend by Friday. And then she'll go back to that village by herself, with a guide who has excellent reviews, and do real damage — to the plant, to the people, to herself." He turned the recorder over in his hand. "I'd rather have her where I can see her."

"Conditional admission, then," Liu said. "A crash course in not being a colonial cautionary tale."

"Two provisional years." He was already counting it off on his fingers, the way he did when a thing had finished forming in him without his noticing. "A seminar on reciprocity. Weekly mentorship. A reflective journal where she takes that tattoo story apart, fault line by fault line, until she can see every place she gave away her own consent and every place she's planning to spend someone else's. And a shadow placement in the community garden, so she watches plants make people well without anybody being robbed to do it."

"Add a rotation in a pharmacology lab," Liu said. "Nothing cures miracle fever like a toxicity screen."

"Done."

"And the plant?" She folded her arms. "She's going to ask to go back for it on day one. Do you forbid the trip?"

This was the part he had already decided and was only now hearing himself decide. "No. Forbid it and I've made it holy. She'll spend the whole two years straining toward the thing I told her she couldn't have." He set the recorder down. "I'll do the opposite. I'll make the plant an *assignment*. Let her chase it — properly, on a short leash, with a notebook and a permit and somebody watching. I'd put my pension on that village miracle turning out to be something already sitting in every database we own. And the morning she finds out she's chased a sunrise will teach her more than I could in three semesters." He almost smiled. "The kindest thing I can do for that young woman is help her be wrong *carefully*."

Liu studied him. "You're enjoying this."

"I'm a little bit enjoying this."

He drew a legal pad toward him and began a reading list — the foundational ethnobotany, the consent literature, and, because she would be working the region whether he liked it or not, the department's own work on it: the two careful papers a colleague named Jones had written years ago on the food and medicine plants of exactly that corner of the Amazon. He underlined Jones twice, for no reason he could have given, and moved on.

The offer letter took them the rest of the afternoon. It was Liu who named it, half as a joke, in the subject line of the draft — *From Tattoo to PhD Program* — and the joke was good enough that neither of them took it out. McClatchey signed it the next morning, before the department chair could hear that he'd let a young woman with a brand tattooed on her forehead in through the side door, and ask him, with reason, what on earth he thought he was doing.

## Chapter 3 — The Miracle Plant

She kept the bargain. Badly, at first — the reflective journal began as four sullen sentences a week — and then, almost against her will, less badly. She pruned kale in the community garden and learned the names of the women who ran it. She sat through the reciprocity seminar with her arms folded and absorbed more than her posture admitted. Through all of it the plant stayed lit inside her like a pilot light, low and constant, waiting.

McClatchey kept his half too. He did not forbid the trip; in her second year, once she'd earned it, he assigned it. Three days, supervised, with a permit and a single rule printed at the top of the itinerary in a font she found insulting: *Identify. Verify. Obtain consent. Collect nothing.* She signed it the way you sign anything standing between you and what you want.

She went back wearing the village's own mark. She had not expected that to move her, and it did — walking up the trail with NIKE still riding her forehead, returning to the only people on earth who thought it was simply her name. Héctor met her at the river. The children remembered her. An old woman touched the tattoo and said something approving that Héctor declined to translate.

She had learned enough Quechua to ask it herself, and she was proud of that, and she used it. *Can I see the plant that lets you live strong?*

Elder Mayta smiled at her — a long, kind, unhurried smile — and sent a boy off down the path. They waited. Smoke coiled in the thatch. The boy came back with a tray, and on the tray, heaped and ordinary, were berries: marble-sized, purplish-red, sweating faintly in the heat.

Nike looked at them and the bottom went out of the afternoon.

She knew them. She had keyed this very plant in a teaching lab the semester before, resentful the whole hour, certain the exercise was beneath someone with a destiny. *Myrciaria dubia.* Camu-camu. A floodplain shrub with the vitamin C of a pharmacy aisle, eaten fresh in season, dried for the floods, described in the literature so many times over that the citations had citations. There was nothing to bring to the civilized world. The civilized world had a folder on it.

"This?" she said, and heard her own voice come out small. "This is it?"

Héctor translated her face more than her word. Mayta answered without offense, as though she had asked something reasonable.

"A fish does not call water rare," Héctor rendered, after a moment. "We eat these in the season and dry them for the floods. Why would you chase the strange when what keeps you grows along the trail?"

She stood in the cookhouse and did the arithmetic of her own folly. She had crossed a hemisphere. She had learned a language. She had let a brand be cut into her face and then gone to a lawyer and *kept* it, built a legend on it, leveraged it into a graduate program over the better judgment of everyone but one stubborn old professor — all to carry home a fruit that any sophomore could name and any database already held. The miracle she had flown all this way for had been sitting in her own lab notebook, marked down in her own handwriting, the semester she was too important to pay attention.

*How could I have been so naive.* It arrived not as a phrase but as a kind of heat in the face, the specific scald of having been certain and wrong in front of people too gracious to enjoy it.

She did the assignment correctly, because she had nothing left to be grand about. She recorded the preparation methods. She pressed a voucher specimen. She did not taste the berries until Mayta offered, and then she did, and they were sour and bright and unremarkable and the best thing she had ever eaten. Under a sky thick with stars she told Héctor, quietly, the only true sentence she had managed all day: that the miracle had never been the plant. It had been learning how to ask a better question.

It should have ended there, clean, a young woman humbled into wisdom on a starlit trail. But she lay awake that night in the cookhouse and felt the lesson turn over and harden into something with an edge. If she could be *this* wrong — this loud, this sure, this far from home — then the wrongness was not hers alone. Someone had handed her the rope. Her advisors had watched her sharpen her certainty for months and let her carry it across a continent. McClatchey had *known*; he'd as good as said so, signing that insulting itinerary. They had all been so careful with her, and not one of them had been careful enough to stop her.

The thought had a warmth in it she mistook for clarity. She would carry it home and aim it, and she would not aim it at herself.

## Chapter 4 — The Blow-Up

She came home from Peru with a humility that lasted nearly three weeks.

The trouble with a new tool is that you want to use it on everything, and she had come back holding a fine one — the habit of asking what a confident answer leaves out. She reread Jones's two assigned papers, the foundational surveys of the region's food and medicine plants that everyone cited and no one questioned, and where a year ago she'd have been impressed, she now saw what she had just been so expensively cured of. A capable outsider had gone in, asked the people what they used, written down what they said, and called it the record. Nowhere in forty pages did he appear to wonder what they hadn't told him. It looked like complacency. It looked, she decided, like a man who had never once been made a fool of, and so had never learned to distrust an answer.

She did not notice that she was, at that very moment, certain of a great deal she had not checked. The lesson of the camu-camu had left one blind spot, and it was shaped exactly like herself.

She brought it up in his seminar. She meant to be pointed and became, somewhere in the second sentence, something past pointed — the data was an artifact of who was asking; the whole celebrated survey was a comfortable outsider's version of a people with every reason to manage what he saw; the word that finally came out of her was *colonial*, and it landed in the room like a dropped tray. Twelve students found things to look at. Dr. Jones did not flinch. He let her finish, which was worse than being cut off, and then said, mildly, that it was an interesting hypothesis and she should come to his office if she meant it.

She meant it. She was there within the hour, still warm.

He did not discipline her, which she had half wanted; he waved her to a chair across a desk drifted with offprints. He was older than his voice in lecture, unhurried, a man who had clearly been shouted at before and survived.

"You think my data is biased," he said. "Say why."

"Because you took their word for it." The heat was still in her. "You walked in as an outsider and assumed the answers you got were the answers there were."

"That's an assumption about my fieldwork." Something almost amused crossed his face. "You've picked the wrong man to call a cowboy. I haven't set foot in that region in twenty years. The recent boots-on-the-ground work out there was Halloran's, not mine — go shout at him." He said it lightly, a door closing on a subject, and she filed it away as a man stepping neatly out of the line of fire.

"That doesn't make the data clean. It makes it old *and* biased."

"It might." He leaned back. "Here is your difficulty, Ms.—" he glanced at the brand on her forehead and chose, with visible care, "—Nike. You don't have a finding. You have a suspicion. I had them too at your age, about my own elders, and most of them were just resentment in a lab coat. A suspicion is a feeling with ambition. It is not science until you've built something that could prove it *wrong*."

"So you want me to drop it."

"On the contrary." And here he did the thing she would not forgive him for, for some time: he was fair. "I want you to *do* it. Don't stand in my seminar announcing that the record is corrupt. Design the study that would actually test whether it is — a real research program, one that an ethics board won't laugh out of the room and a committee can't dismiss as a grudge. Show me how you'd measure the thing you're so sure of." He let that sit. "Bring me that proposal, and it does two jobs at once. It tests your idea, and it ends your provisional standing — because you'll have proven you can do the exact thing you're accusing me of failing at. Bring me a polemic instead, or bring me nothing, and we will part company without hard feelings."

"And if I'm right? If your data really is bent?"

"Then I will have spent thirty years being wrong," he said, without any particular distress, "and I would genuinely like to know. That's the part you haven't understood yet. I'd rather be corrected than comfortable. The deadline is the end of term. Surprise me."

She walked out furious, and stayed furious all the way down the stairs and half the way home, because he had refused to be a villain and handed her a deadline instead. He had taken the most satisfying feeling she owned and told her it was worthless until she did the work — the work, the careful, slow, unglamorous work, the thing the camu-camu had been screaming at her about under all those stars.

She was nearly to her apartment before the first useful thought arrived, cold and small and entirely her own. He had assigned the class two papers. But he had written more than two. She had seen the list once, on a library terminal — a longer bibliography under his name, years older, and one title near the bottom that had nothing to do with plants at all.

## Chapter 5 — Yellow Names

The paper was where she'd remembered it, near the bottom of a bibliography two decades older than the surveys he assigned. It was not about plants. It was about names — the naming customs of the river people, written by a younger Jones she didn't recognize, a man who had clearly loved the place before he learned to be distant about it.

She read it twice. In it he reported, with unmistakable pride, that over nine years on the river he had been taken into two villages and given a name in each. He listed them. They were birds — small, bright, particular birds — and she sat very still when she understood that both of them were yellow.

The man who had waved her off an hour ago — *I haven't set foot in that region in twenty years, go shout at Halloran* — had once spent nine years there, deep enough to be named twice. It was not quite a lie; the nine years were long ago, and "twenty years" was true on its face. But it was a self he had folded up and put away, and the folded version did not match the proud young one in her hands. Two accounts under the same name, and they disagreed. It was the first hard thing she had found all term. Not a feeling. A fact with two sources that wouldn't line up.

The yellow birds snagged on something and would not come loose, and after a while she knew what it was. She called Héctor.

He was slow to answer the question, and slower to answer it honestly, and she understood by now that the slowness was the answer beginning. Long ago, he told her — and the river villages still, the ones that hadn't changed — they named men for animals and women for plants. That much was in the paper. What was not in the paper, what no one wrote down because no one needed to, was the color. A yellow creature, a yellow flower, and the name meant the person was not to be trusted. A little yellow, a little. Very yellow, not at all. They never explained this to outsiders. They didn't have to. When a stranger arrived and said his name, everyone who mattered already knew how much of themselves to put in front of him.

Her own village, Héctor said, had given the animal-and-plant names up a generation back — partly because the color had made them treacherous to bestow, a quarrel folded into a gift — and taken the tattoo instead. A name with no color in it. The practice had ended. The knowledge had not.

She sat with the phone dark in her hand and felt the whole shape of it arrive at once.

Jones had carried two yellow-bird names through nine years of fieldwork and never once known what they said about him. And the villages had known — every day, every question, every plant he asked after — exactly how much to set in front of a man the names marked as untrustworthy. His celebrated surveys might not be biased the way she had shouted in his seminar. He had not been lazy, or arrogant, or too quick to believe. He had been *managed*. The record was not a comfortable outsider's careless version of the truth; it was a careful people's deliberate edit of it, keyed to a rule written into his own names, in a paper he had published with pride and could not read.

He had been labeled by a code he couldn't see. Her hand was at her own forehead before she knew it had moved.

And then a smaller, stranger thought, the kind that comes in uninvited and stays: what color was *Nike*? None. A brand was no animal and no flower; it sat nowhere in the system at all. The rule had a slot for trusted and a slot for not, and no slot for her. She was the one kind of stranger the code could not read either — illegible in the other direction. It unsettled her more than she wanted it to, and she set it aside for later, which is where the true things wait.

The idea was beautiful. That was exactly what frightened her. The last beautiful, certain idea she'd had she had carried across a hemisphere to find it sitting in her own lab notebook in her own handwriting. A suspicion with a mechanism was more dangerous than a suspicion without one, not less — it felt so much more like proof, and proof was the one thing she still did not have. She had a man's two yellow names, a custom told to her over a bad phone line by a friend who'd rather not have, and a story so satisfying she wanted to skip straight to being right.

*Build something that could prove it wrong.* For the first time she heard the sentence not as a hoop but as the whole of the job. She turned to a clean page and, where a month ago she'd have written an accusation, she began instead to write a question — one she could actually put to the test.

## Chapter 6 — Designing the Test

She built it over a week of evenings, and what she built was a chain.

She was not going to prove anything about Jones in a single stroke; she had learned what single strokes were worth. Instead she made three tests, each one a link, each one a thing that had to hold before the next one meant anything. The first asked whether the color in the names was real at all — not just her own village's quirk but a rule shared across the river: sample the names in several villages, map their colors, and test, carefully, whether a yellow-named person was held less trustworthy than the commonest non-yellow color. The second asked whether the villages *assigned* the color by how they felt — whether a newcomer's disposition and his name's color lined up, a question a plain contingency table could put a number to. The third asked the only one that finally mattered: whether what a village *told* an outsider depended on the color of his name.

Break the first link and there was no code. Break the second and the code wasn't about trust. Break the third and trust didn't reach the data. If any of the three failed, her case against the surveys failed with it — and she found, to her own surprise, that she liked that. A study that could collapse in three named places was a study that might be believed if it didn't.

She took it to McClatchey and Liu, and they did not congratulate her, which she now understood was the same as taking her seriously.

"Say the third test comes back the way you want," Liu said. "Yellow-named men get told less. Is the name *doing* that, or is the name just a tag on a distrust that's doing it? You can't shave the name off a man and run him again."

Nike had the answer ready, because she'd lost a night to it. "I can't say the name causes the silence. Only that it marks it — reliably, from the outside, where I can read it and they can't see me reading. That's a sign of a judgment I can't measure directly." She wrote it into the margin as she said it. "It's enough to call the data filtered. It isn't enough to call the man careless. Those are different claims and I'm only allowed the first one."

McClatchey had been quiet, turning the pages. "How many outsiders does a village name in a generation? Two? Four? Your second and third tests have a sample the size of a dinner party."

That was the wound, and she knew it. "Then I can't run it in one village. I run the region — many villages pooled, and the named outsiders that anyone still remembers reconstructed from the elders and from whatever was written down." She didn't look up. "Jones among them. He's not just who I'm testing. He's one of my data points." It was a strange sentence to say out loud, and saying it steadied her. "Sparse cells. Exact tests, not the comfortable ones. A small, honest number instead of a large dishonest one."

"And the part you're walking around," Liu said, gently, which was how Liu said the hardest things. "You are an outsider, proposing to study a secret that is *defined* by being kept from outsiders. The minute they know what you're measuring, they can manage you precisely the way you believe they managed him. How do you ask about a hidden rule without the asking hiding it?"

The room held still. It was the brand thought again, the one she'd set aside for later, and later had arrived. "I don't know yet," she said. "But I'm not nowhere. I'm not yellow. I'm not anything — the code has no slot for me. That's either a door or a wall, and finding out which is its own first experiment." She let it stand. It was the most honest thing in the proposal, and the least finished.

McClatchey went to his shelf then and came back with a worn paperback she half-recognized, and set it in front of her: *Coming of Age in Samoa.* "You're not the first to suspect that the people across the notebook were telling the visitor a story." He told her the rest — the famous record, and the man who came along decades later and charged that Mead's young informants had simply told a curious outsider what she wanted to hear; and how the field had argued it for forty years and never, in the end, agreed. "That's the lesson inside the gift," he said. "The most famous case we have of an outsider being misled has no verdict, because proving you were misled is that hard. If you want to do better than a shouting match across a seminar table, your design has to be better than Mead's accusers ever managed."

She took the book. She would cite it not as a banner but as a warning — the high-water mark of exactly how far suspicion gets you without a mechanism you can test.

By the end of the week the proposal was strong nearly everywhere, and the one place it wasn't was the place the whole story had been about. To learn whether a people withheld from a stranger they didn't trust, she would have to keep from them what she had really come to learn. She would have to study concealment by concealing — walk into villages with a friendly cover question and measure, in secret, what they declined to give a woman who hadn't told them why she was asking.

It was the camu-camu in a new coat. The thrilling shortcut. The thing that worked, paid for by people who had never agreed to pay. She had designed a study she was proud of and could not, as it stood, run without becoming the thing the study was about.

The deadline was three weeks off. She put on her shoes and went to find Liu.

## Chapter 7 — The Reckoning

Liu listened to the whole knot without interrupting, which was how Nike knew it was a real one.

"There's only one way out of it," Liu said at last. "You stop concealing. You go in and you tell them exactly what you're measuring and why, and you ask their leave, and you let them be partners in it rather than subjects of it."

"And if telling them collapses the thing I'm trying to measure? If they manage me the moment they know?"

"Then it collapses, and you write an honest null instead of a dishonest finding, and you'll have learned something true about how hard the question is." Liu shrugged, not unkindly. "That's the whole trade. Can you live on the right side of it?"

She could. The camu-camu had taught her the going rate for a thrilling shortcut, and she had decided never again to pay it with money that wasn't hers.

But that left the harder question, the one no resolve could answer by itself: why on earth would they say yes? Why would any village agree to let an outsider make its oldest secret visible to the world? A hundred strangers had come up that river in a century with gifts and good reasons, and the people had handed each of them a careful, partial, perfectly polite version of the truth and watched them leave satisfied. She had nothing to offer that had not been offered and refused before.

Her hand went to her forehead, and this time she let it stay there.

She had carried the tattoo for two years as a joke at her own expense — the brand, the souvenir of the night she'd been fool enough to pass out at a ceremony. To the village it had never once been a joke. Héctor told her what it was, slowly, because it was not a thing said lightly: a vow, made in the ceremony, binding in both directions. And there were stories — old ones, and not gentle — of the ones who had taken the mark and then gone home and cut it away. What the cutting said about them. What it had cost. The bad luck the guide had warned her of, that first bewildered morning, given at last a face and a name. *It will bring you more distress than you can imagine. You must leave it as it is.*

She had left it as it was. She had worn it home, and gone to a lawyer, and made it true, and then climbed back up the trail still wearing it, the only outsider in living memory who had kept the mark instead of erasing it the moment she was clear of them. The code had no slot for her — she was no animal and no flower, no color at all. But the people had a place for her that the code didn't: she was the stranger who had kept faith. That was not nothing. To them it was very nearly everything.

So she made the call — a satellite line, a borrowed laptop, Héctor in the frame and the elders gathered behind him in the cookhouse light — and she told them the truth. All of it. The named outsiders. The yellow birds. The professor whose careful surveys the whole world trusted, and what she had come to believe those surveys were missing, and why. And then what she wanted, which was not to carry their secret off down the river but to help them show it, on their own terms — to prove, so that it could never again be pretended otherwise, that the *yes* a village gave a curious stranger had never been the whole of what the village knew. So that no next professor, and no company with a wellness initiative and a fleet of lawyers, could ever again mistake what they had been handed for what these people actually had.

She asked their consent. She asked them to be the authors of it beside her, to keep the final say over what was shown and what stayed in the dark.

The elders did not answer quickly, and she made herself sit in the silence and not fill it, because she had learned at last that the silence was the answer beginning. When the oldest of them spoke, it was not about the design or the data. It was about the mark. He spoke of the ones who had cut it away, and of what was owed to one who hadn't, and Héctor's translation came haltingly because some of it did not have English in it. They would consider it, the elder said. They would set the terms — what she could ask, where she could ask it, what would never leave the village. And she said yes before he had finished, because terms set by the people you study are the exact opposite of the thing she had nearly done, and she wanted to live as far from that thing as she could get.

She closed the laptop and sat a while in the dark of her apartment.

She had come into the year certain she would expose a careless man, and she was leaving it holding something she could only have built because she — once, by an enormous margin, the most naive person in the entire story — had kept, first by accident and then by stubborn choice, a mark that made her trustworthy to the one people on earth whose trust the study could not be done without. The brand that had gotten her laughed into a graduate program had become the credential that made the work honest. The proposal in her bag was not clever anymore. Clever was what she'd been in the seminar, shouting *colonial* at a man who'd forgotten more fieldwork than she'd done. This was the other thing. This was earned.

There were three days left. In the morning she printed it, squared the pages, and carried it across campus to a door drifted with offprints.

## Chapter 8 — How Scientists Write Research Proposals

Jones read it at his desk — the one drifted with offprints — the way he read student work, which is to say quickly, with a pen, prepared to be patient. Somewhere on the second page he stopped reading that way.

She watched him slow down. He went back and reread the part where the three tests were laid out as a chain, each one able to break and take her case down with it. He paused a long while over the margin note about cause and marker — *enough to call the data filtered, not enough to call the man careless* — and tapped it once with the pen as though confirming it was load-bearing. He read the Mead paragraph and made a small sound that might have been amusement. And when he reached the section on consent, on the village as partners, on terms set by the people being studied, he set the pen down entirely, which she had come to understand from McClatchey was a thing these men did when a thing had finished forming in them.

"You actually solved it," he said. "The access problem. You didn't trick your way past it. You made it the design." He looked up. "How did you get them to agree to be studied on the one thing they've spent generations not telling anyone?"

This was the part she had dreaded, because it was the part where she put him in it.

"That's the next section," she said.

He read it. She watched his face as he came to the named outsiders, to the color of names, to the reconstruction of the men a village remembered and what each had been given. And then to the two names. His two. Copied out of his own linguistics paper of thirty years ago and set down beside the code, in her neat hand, so there could be no mistaking what they were. Two yellow birds. The mark, in the people's own grammar, of a man not to be trusted — worn by him through nine years he had been so proud of that he'd published the names like medals.

The office went very quiet. Outside, somewhere, a leaf blower started and was mercifully far away.

She did not gloat. She had rehearsed not gloating until it was real. "They knew the whole time," she said, gently, the way you hand someone a diagnosis. "Every plant you asked after. They weren't careless with you and you weren't careless with them. They were *careful* — they always knew exactly how much to set in front of a man whose names said what yours said. The surveys aren't wrong. They're just not the whole of what those villages knew. And nobody could have told you, because the one paper that would have explained it was the one you wrote yourself."

Jones was still for a long time. She let him be.

She had braced for the other version of this man — the one who'd defend the thirty years, who'd find the flaw in her cells, who'd point out, correctly, that two data points are not a finding. He had every one of those moves available and she had answers to most of them. He used none.

"I loved that river," he said at last, and it came out of the distant theorist in a younger voice, the one from the paper. "I thought they'd named me because they loved me back." He turned a page he wasn't reading. There was grief in it, real and brief, the particular grief of a man recounting thirty years and finding a seam he'd never seen. And then — she watched it happen — the grief gave way to the thing he'd promised her in this office weeks ago, the thing she had not believed he meant. He looked, of all the impossible things, *delighted*. "Do you understand what you've done? There's a village in my second season — Sapani — that I always wrote up as reticent. Traditional. Closed. I built a whole footnote excusing the thinness of that site." He laughed, once, without much breath in it. "It wasn't the village that was thin. It was me. You've just handed me thirty years of footnotes that say something I couldn't read." He shook his head. "This is the most useful thing anyone has done to my work in a very long time."

"So it passes," she said.

"It more than passes." He squared her pages, the way she had squared them that morning. "Your provisional years are over. You've done the thing you accused me of failing at, and you did it to *me*, which took a kind of nerve I'd like to think I had at your age and suspect I didn't." He set the proposal down between them, evenly, like something jointly owned. "But that's the small part. There's a course the department's been circling for a decade and never had the nerve to build — not the seminar where we hand you a template and a deadline, the real one. How a scientist actually *makes* a proposal. How you turn a feeling into a thing that can be proven wrong." He tapped the pages. "We've never had an example clean enough to teach from. We do now. I'd like you at the table when we design it. Not as a student in the room. As the person who wrote the thing we'll teach."

She had come into this program by leaving a voicemail begging a man not to look at her grades. She was being asked, two years later, to help decide what the next students' grades would mean.

Walking out, she caught her reflection in the dark glass of a display case in the hall — the word still there above her eyes, blunt and blue and permanent. *Nike.* She had worn it for two years as the punchline of the worst decision of her life, and it had taken her this long to remember, or perhaps to deserve to notice, that the brand on the shirt had been named for a goddess, and that the word those Greeks had given her stood for *victory*. The villagers had read a stranger's name off a label and gotten, by pure accident, the truest thing anyone had ever called her. She had spent two years earning the accident.

She went to find McClatchey, who had wanted only to help her be wrong carefully, and who would want to know — would already half know, the insufferable man — that she had finally managed to be right the same way.
