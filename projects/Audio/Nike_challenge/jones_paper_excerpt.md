# Ch.5 artifact — excerpt from Jones's early paper (for Kim to set with the two bird figures)

*Mock citation (adjust journal/volume/year/pages to taste; ~30 years before the story):*
**A. R. Jones (1992).** "To Be Named by the River: Personal Names, Trust, and the
Transmission of Plant Knowledge Among the Quechua of the Lower Napo Drainage."
*Journal of Ethnobiology* 12(2): 187–205. *(excerpt, pp. 198–199)*

---

In nine years on the river I learned that a botanist's notebook fills only as fast as his
welcome allows. The plants of this floodplain are not secrets — the people live among them
as we live among our furniture — but their *uses* are held close, and a stranger is told the
uses he is judged ready to be told. I came slowly to understand that my catalogue of food and
medicine plants — the camu-camu eaten by the handful when the water is high, the barks steeped
against fever, the leaves a mother knows by her grandmother's name — was less a record I had
taken than a gift I had been given, in the measure my hosts saw fit to give it.

That measure, I have come to believe, is bound up with names. Among these communities a person
is known by the name of a living thing: a man is named for an animal, a woman for a plant. To
be given such a name is to be made legible — to become someone the village can place — and a
stranger who has earned a name has earned, with it, some portion of the knowledge that passes
only between the named. I do not think it is too much to say that everything of value in the
present survey was told to me *after* I had been named, and by people who had had a hand in
naming me.

It was the great fortune of my working life to be named not once but twice — at Sapani in my
third season, and at Yanayacu in my seventh. Both names, as it happened, were birds (Figs. 1
and 2), and both — a coincidence that has always delighted me — were yellow. I have asked,
over the years and always gently, why these particular birds and not others were chosen for
me, and have been answered each time only with the smile a people keep for a question that does
not need answering. I have learned to let the matter rest. The names were given with evident
warmth; I have worn them with more pride than I have worn any degree, and I record them here in
gratitude to the two villages that, against every reason a river has to distrust a stranger,
decided to know me.

*Notes for Kim:* the two vernacular names (*Sirpi*, *Tankáru*) are invented and color-free on
purpose — the yellow code is never spoken to outsiders, so the names carry no hint of it; Jones
only remarks on the "coincidence." Swap the species/common names if they fit your two bird
illustrations better (both are real, genuinely yellow Amazonian birds — one larger/sleeker, one
smaller/rounder, to match the two species in your image). "Sapani" is the same village Jones
later writes up as "reticent" in Ch.8 — a quiet plant for the payoff.
