# Sea Otter Population Data from Jones Refuge Reports (1948–1974)

## Methodology

### Source Material
52 data records extracted from 34 of 38 annual/quarterly reports by Robert D. Jones Jr., 
Izembek National Wildlife Refuge and Aleutian Islands NWR, 1948–1974.

### Species Identification
Earlier validation (random sampling of 9 reports across 1948–1974) confirmed that 
unqualified "otter" in wildlife survey context refers to sea otter (*Enhydra lutris*) 
in 100% of cases. Land otter (*Lutra canadensis*) is always explicitly qualified. 
This convention holds throughout the corpus.

### Data Classification
Records are classified by observation method:

- **aerial_survey** (7): Counts from aircraft overflights, most systematic
- **survey_count** (16): Boat-based or ground surveys, often multi-site
- **census_form** (3): Standardized FWS wildlife census form entries
- **observation** (10): Incidental sightings with counts
- **estimate** (4): Population estimates derived from survey extrapolation
- **harvest** (2): ADF&G commercial/experimental harvest totals
- **mortality** (2): Dead animal recovery counts
- **historical** (1): Cross-referenced data from earlier surveys
- **qualitative** (7): Narrative observations without specific counts

### Geographic Scope
The reports cover two primary regions:

1. **Western Aleutians** (Amchitka, Rat Island, Tongue Island) — large resident populations
2. **Eastern Aleutians/Alaska Peninsula** (Shumagin Islands, Sanak Islands, Unimak, Izembek Bay) — smaller, expanding populations

## Key Findings

### 1. Amchitka Core Population (Western Aleutians)
The Amchitka area held the largest known sea otter concentration throughout the study period:

| Year | Location | Count | Method |
|------|----------|------:|--------|
| 1949 | Amchitka north (East Cape–Alcat Pt) | 526 | Aerial survey |
| 1949 | Amchitka south (1st flight) | 314 | Aerial survey |
| 1949 | Amchitka south (2nd flight + Bird Rocks) | 559 | Aerial survey |
| 1949 | Rat Island | 279 | Aerial survey |
| 1952 | Amchitka offshore rocks | 111 | Observation |
| 1953 | Amchitka (historical ref) | 3,420 | Historical |
| 1956 | Tongue Island area | 3,500 | Estimate |
| 1959 | Off Eddy Island | 400 | Observation (single pod) |

The 1953 report cites a 1949 survey total of 3,420 at Amchitka — a much higher figure 
than the partial aerial counts in the 1949 report itself. The 1956 estimate of ~3,500 
at Tongue Island suggests the population was stable or slowly growing through this period.

In 1971, the Cannikin nuclear test (5 megatons) killed "perhaps several hundred" sea 
otters on Amchitka — a significant but not catastrophic loss for a population of this size.

### 2. Shumagin Islands (Eastern Range)

| Year | Location | Count | Method |
|------|----------|------:|--------|
| 1953 | Shumagin Islands total | 633 | Survey count |
| 1954 | Twelve Fathom Strait | ~200 | Estimate |
| 1960 | Simeonof Island | 26 | Aerial survey |
| 1960 | Little Koniuji Island | 110 | Aerial survey |

The apparent decline from 633 (1953) to 136 total (1960) at the Shumagins warrants 
investigation. Possible explanations include seasonal/methodological differences rather 
than an actual decline, since the 1953 count was a comprehensive boat survey while the 
1960 count was a quick aerial survey.

### 3. Izembek/Cold Bay Area (Range Expansion)
The most striking pattern in the data is the colonization of Izembek Bay:

| Year | Location | Count | Notes |
|------|----------|------:|-------|
| 1955 | Cold Bay entrance | 1 | First record near Cold Bay |
| 1960 | Izembek Bay (Grant's Point) | 1 | Single animal |
| 1961 | Morzhovoi Bay | 1 | Single sighting |
| 1962 | Izembek/Morzhovoi Bays | 9 | Multiple sightings |
| 1962 | Middle Lagoon | 3 | Repeated sightings |
| 1963 | Izembek NWR | 13 | FWS census form |
| 1964 | Izembek NWR | 23 | FWS census form |
| 1965 | Izembek NWR | 25 | FWS census form |
| 1966 | Near Izembek | — | "Large number" noted (ship grounding hazard) |
| 1967 | Izembek Bay | — | "Sufficiently common"; family groups seen |

This sequence documents a clear colonization event: from a single animal in 1955 to 
an established breeding population by 1967, with roughly exponential early growth 
(1→9→13→23→25 over 1960–1965). By 1966–1967, the population was large enough 
that a ship grounding posed a fuel-spill threat and that observers routinely encountered 
family groups.

### 5. Management Actions
The reports document three major management interventions:

1. **Translocation attempts** (1951, 1955): Efforts to capture live sea otters for 
   relocation, with mixed success.
2. **Experimental/commercial harvest** (1962, 1968): ADF&G harvested 425 sea otters 
   from the central Aleutians in fall 1968 (62% female). This indicates the population 
   was considered large enough for sustainable take.
3. **Cannikin nuclear test** (1971): Several hundred sea otters killed on Amchitka.

## Data Quality Notes

1. **Comparability**: Counts from different methods (aerial, boat, ground, census forms) 
   are not directly comparable. Aerial surveys typically undercount.
2. **Seasonal variation**: Counts were taken at various times of year; sea otter 
   distribution shifts seasonally.
3. **Geographic mixing**: Some year-to-year changes reflect different survey areas 
   rather than population trends.
4. **OCR limitations**: Some data from structured FWS census tables (1960s–1970s) was 
   garbled by OCR; census form values for 1963–1965 should be verified against originals.
5. **Gap years**: No sea otter data in reports for 1948 (first report, focused on 
   refuge establishment), 1950 (behavioral observations only), 1957 (no report), 
   1969, 1972, 1973 (no reports).
