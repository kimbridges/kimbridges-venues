## The story to build the chapter around [V: Kim]

Kim's teaching anecdote — and the spine of this chapter. In his UH
introductory biology lectures (majors and non-majors, very large classes), one
of the first things he told students was that they might see him do something
strange while crossing campus: he would stop at the sight of a pair of mynahs,
turn to face them, tuck his hands into his armpits, and **nod his head three
times.** He was talking to them — saying, in effect, *this is my territory.*

The payoff is the test of it. A **visiting** pair would usually fly off. The
**resident** pair would hold their ground and **nod back.** The mynah's own
territorial display, returned to a human who had issued the challenge in their
own body language.

Kim's framing — the line the chapter should earn its way to:
> "How often do you get to talk to an animal and get an authentic reply?"

Why it's the spine, not a footnote: it turns the chapter from a description of
an introduced bird into a first-person account of reading and being read by
one. It is unambiguously Kim-observed, it teaches real animal behavior
(territoriality, ritualized display), and it carries the warmth that lifts the
whole book above a field guide.

## The "wrong species": the talking-mynah thread [V: Kim] [V: lit]

A second thread for the chapter, and a natural companion to the territory
story — both are about what this bird *can and can't* do.

People sometimes ask whether Honolulu's mynah can talk, remembering a talking
mynah from television. It can't, much — because the famous talker is a
**different bird**: the **Hill Mynah** (*Gracula religiosa*), a different
genus from our **Common Mynah** (*Acridotheres tristis*). The hill mynah is
ranked among the best talking birds in the world, rivalling the African grey
parrot for clarity of human speech. [V: lit] Honolulu got the common mynah,
not the hill mynah — so the bird everyone *sees* is not the bird everyone
*heard about*.

Kim's personal beat (handle gently — it's a tender memory): Kim once met one
of the top talking-star mynahs. He happened to see it on what proved to be the
**last time it ever talked** — the bird died in the night after his visit.
[V: Kim, personal]

The literary beat: **Aldous Huxley's last novel, *Island* (1962),** a utopia in
which trained mynahs call out **"Attention!"** and **"Here and now"** as a
public reminder to stay present — a Buddhist-tinged device of the book. [V: lit]
Kim knew the book as a student and carried a small, lasting regret that
Honolulu "got the wrong species" — the bird on every lawn is the mute cousin
of Huxley's attentive mynah. That regret is the chapter's wistful note, and it
pairs against the territory story's delight: one mynah encounter gives you an
authentic reply; the other never will.

## Verification results

**Introduction — CONFIRMED [V: lit].** Ed Bryan, *Check List and Summary of
Hawaiian Birds* (1958): "Mina or Mynah, *Piha‘e-kelo*. Intro. from India,
1865. Estab. on all main Haw'n Is. Native of India, Burma, and Thailand."
Primary source, supplied by Kim. So: 1865, from India, pest-control era —
**three decades before the Hui Manu (1930)**, and not an aesthetic
introduction. **Hui Manu thread stays silent in this chapter.** Hawaiian name
to use: **Piha‘e-kelo.**

**Head-bob — PARTIALLY confirmed; read the caveat [V: lit] + [V: Kim].** The
head-bob is a real, documented common-mynah display — but the general
literature ties it most explicitly to **song and courtship** (head bowing and
bobbing with fluffed plumage, accompanied by calls). Mynas are *also*
intensely territorial (violent battles over nest sites). What the general
sources do NOT cleanly nail is the specific reading in Kim's story — the
head-bob as a **territorial challenge returned to an intruder.**
  - The honest position: the behavior is real; its territorial *function in
    this context* rests on **Kim's own field evidence**, which is actually the
    stronger proof — visiting pairs fled, the resident pair held and nodded
    back. That **differential response is the behavioral assay** (cf. Kim's
    meta-observation #4, verification-as-test-case-selection): the response
    itself distinguishes a territorial reading from a chance one.
  - Before publishing the specific "territorial display" wording, worth one
    look at a primary ethology source (Birds of the World *Common Myna*
    behavior account; Sengupta's myna studies) to confirm the head-bob is
    named as agonistic/territorial and not solely courtship. Until then the
    chapter can lean on Kim's assay and describe the behavior precisely
    without over-claiming the textbook label.

## Communal roost (added to draft 2026-06-04) [V: Kim] [V: lit]
Kim's observation: pairs by day, very approachable; toward evening they fly
from daytime locations to a few big trees and cluster in large numbers — in
the right spot you're surprised how many fly past on the way. They chatter a
long time before settling; very loud; a "mynah tree" you would not want in
your yard. Literature backs all of it: alone/in pairs by day, communal
evening roosts of scores to thousands (mainly the non-breeding season),
noisy chatter continuing past nightfall; territories defended in pairs in the
breeding season. (Cornell Lab; Birds of the World; Wikipedia *Common myna*.)
The day-territory / night-communal contrast is a nice biological pairing with
the territory story above.
