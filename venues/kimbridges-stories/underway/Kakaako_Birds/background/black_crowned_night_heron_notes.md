# Black-crowned Night Heron / ‘auku‘u — working notes

## Sources (literature-confirmed claims)
- **Native / indigenous; Hawai‘i's only native heron.** *Nycticorax nycticorax
  hoactli* — indigenous to Hawai‘i (also found across the Americas and beyond).
  (Bishop Museum RLP monograph BCNH.pdf; DLNR ‘Auku‘u; Kaelepulu Wetland;
  DOFAW.)
- **Diurnal here.** Unlike continental night herons (mostly nocturnal), Hawai‘i
  birds forage by day as well as at dusk/night. (DLNR; To-Hawaii.com.)
- **"Stand and wait" ambush.** Crouched, motionless at the water's edge; stares;
  lunging strike. (Audubon; Wikipedia; HeronConservation.)
- **Diet (opportunistic).** Fish, frogs, crustaceans, insects, mice, lizards,
  snakes, small birds, eggs — INCLUDING the young of other native waterbirds —
  plus carrion/refuse. (Wikipedia; DLNR.) [Kept out of the chapter's main prose
  to hold the café focus; the chick-predation on native waterbirds is a real
  conservation nuance available if a future edit wants it.]
- **Habitat.** Edges of ponds, canals, ditches, shorelines, marshy wetlands,
  mountain streams (breeds to ~300 m). A source explicitly notes they're seen
  "along the canal near Ala Moana Beach Park." (DLNR; To-Hawaii.com.)
- **Foraging spacing.** Mostly SOLITARY forager; maintains exclusive feeding
  territories; loose dispersed aggregations, denser where prey concentrated.
  (Wikipedia; Wilson Journal foraging study, O‘ahu.) → corroborates Kim's
  "spaced out along the waterway, enough for everyone."
- **ID / call.** Stocky, short-necked; adult black crown + back, gray wings,
  pale below, red eye, white head plumes in breeding; juveniles brown-streaked.
  Harsh "quok"/"wok" call. (Audubon; Wikipedia.)

## Kim-observed anchors / the frame [V: Kim]
- A fish-eater that does NOT fly to the ocean (contrast with the white tern);
  found a different strategy at Ala Moana Beach Park.
- The canal running the park's length parallel to Ala Moana Blvd; brackish,
  tidal; ~a foot deep at times; low tide leaves fish trapped in depression
  ponds (unless they sensed the tide). Herons perch the canal sides; plenty of
  fish → patience pays.
- Territoriality generally observed; birds space along the long waterway;
  enough food for all.
- **The "urban café" metaphor** — the canal's formal, uniform habitat is like a
  city's row of cafés; spaced diners, each at its table. Kim's coinage; the
  chapter's spine.

## Thematic notes
- **Native that adapts to urban infrastructure.** Bridges the book's two halves:
  the introduced-birds-adapting-to-the-city theme AND the native section. The
  ‘auku‘u shows nativeness ≠ apartness-from-us — a native reading the city's
  plumbing as a wetland. Good transition chapter within the native cluster.
- **Native cluster now:** White Tern (resident seabird), ‘Auku‘u (resident
  waterbird), Kōlea + ‘Akekeke (migratory), Koloa (endemic — to come). The
  ‘auku‘u is the resident-native fisher of the urban canal.
- **Café metaphor** pairs nicely against the zebra dove's "pavement" and the
  pigeon's "bus stop" — the book's recurring use of human-built settings as the
  birds' habitats.
