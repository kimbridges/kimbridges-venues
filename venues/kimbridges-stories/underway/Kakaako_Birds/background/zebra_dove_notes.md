## Sources (literature-confirmed claims)
- **Introduction 1922; now the most widespread dove.** Released on O‘ahu,
  Moloka‘i, and Maui in 1922, possibly via a Singapore bird dealer; common on
  nearly all main islands by the late 1930s; now occupies every developed
  lowland and is the most widespread/abundant dove on the main islands. Native
  to Southeast Asia. (Bishop Museum RLP monograph ZEBD.pdf; To-Hawaii.com;
  Hawai‘i Birding Trails; Wikipedia.)
- **Ground forager.** Favors bare/sparse ground (dirt, lawns, roadsides); quick,
  deliberate, "rodent-like" scurrying gait; eats small grass/weed seeds and
  small invertebrates. (earthlife.net; BESG; Wikipedia.)
- **Flocking.** Often pairs/small groups; gathers in larger congregations at
  good feeding sites or water. (Kim observes "larger flocks," consistent with
  Hawai‘i's high density + feeding-site congregations; relative to the spotted
  dove it is the more group-prone of the two.)
- **Male display/territory.** Males perform a bowing-cooing courtship display
  and use cooing for pair-bonding and territory. (Lit supports male as the
  displaying/territorial sex; the explicit "aggressive" framing is Kim's
  observation — handled as such.)
- **Cultural — the merbuk.** In Malaysia/SE Asia called merbuk; a prized
  singing cage bird entered in cooing competitions judged on voice. The soft
  rhythmic coo. (Wikipedia; earthlife.net.)
- **Field marks.** Small, slim, long-tailed; fine black barring on neck and
  breast-sides ("zebra"); bluish-gray face; pinkish wash below. Smaller than the
  spotted dove. (Field guides; Wikipedia.)

## Kim-observed anchors [V: Kim]
- The smaller of Honolulu's two doves; found in larger flocks than the spotted.
- On lawns, but also sidewalks and parking lots; a brave little bird that walks
  about intent on food, letting people get close.
- Males can become quite aggressive.
- Sits in the background, not calling attention to itself.
