## Sources (literature-confirmed claims)
- **Native migratory visitor.** *Arenaria interpres*, ‘akekeke — Hawaiian name
  from its rattling call "a-ke-ke-ke." Common winter visitor to Hawai‘i (~Aug to
  Apr/May); breeds on high-Arctic tundra (one of the most northerly-breeding
  shorebirds); returns to the Arctic to breed. Indigenous migratory (NOT
  endemic; not introduced). (DLNR ‘Akekeke; Hawai‘i Birding Trails; Kure Atoll
  Conservancy; To-Hawaii.com.)
- **Migration feats.** Long overwater flights, incl. Alaska→Hawai‘i; >1,000 km/
  day possible. **Juveniles migrate alone:** first flight ~19 days old, then
  fly thousands of miles to nonbreeding grounds ~2 days later, parents already
  departed — youngsters navigate the first migration unaccompanied. (Kure Atoll
  Conservancy; Wikipedia; Audubon.)
- **Stone-turning forage.** Flips stones, shells, seaweed with strong neck/short
  wedge bill to take invertebrates underneath (origin of "turnstone"); diet
  flies/spiders/beetles/grubs + crustaceans, small fish, worms, even eggs.
  Spiny feet grip wet rock; low center of gravity. (Audubon; DLNR; A-Z Animals.)
- **Plumage/habitat.** Small, calico/"tortoiseshell," short bill, bright orange
  legs; non-breeding (what we see here in winter) is duller mottled brown —
  cryptic against coral/rock. Habitats: rocky shores, sandy coast, mudflats,
  intertidal, and plowed fields/lawns near coast; small flocks. (Wikipedia;
  Audubon; To-Hawaii.com.)

## Kim-observed anchors [V: Kim]
- Easy to overlook; only ever ~5 individuals at a time.
- Unlikely setting: the heavily populated sand of **Ala Moana Beach Park**,
  among rocky coral fragments, poking for aquatic food.
- Always on the move; retreats to open sand and even into the human habitat
  (concrete, lawns). True shoreline birds.
- The reader address: "lay on the beach to tan; keep one eye open" to spot them.

## Thematic notes
- **Native section — the migratory natives.** Pairs with the **Pacific
  Golden-Plover / kōlea** (the other Arctic/sub-Arctic migratory shorebird).
  Both are "overlooked lawn/shore birds that are secretly globe-spanning
  travelers" and both are natives that arrive **under their own power** — the
  counter to the carried-in introduced birds. Keep the two adjacent.
- **Native-by-returning** is the chapter's distinctive idea: native not by
  staying (white tern) but by an ancient annual return — a tie to the place
  across deep time, older than the first canoe. Sits between the introduced
  birds (people who came) and the white tern (the place); the migratory natives
  are the threads that connect the islands to the wider world under their own
  power.
- **Thesis fit (overlooked → extraordinary):** cryptic, small, easy to miss,
  yet one of the great travelers — the search-image lesson with a payoff of awe.
