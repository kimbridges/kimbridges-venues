## Sources (literature-confirmed claims)
- **Introduction 1970s, from escaped cage birds.** Established on O‘ahu by the
  early 1970s from escaped cage birds; a flock of 20–25 first noted in a cane
  field near ‘Ewa Beach on 7 Nov 1973; spread to Pearl City/‘Aiea (1983),
  Punalu‘u (1984), Diamond Head (1985), and most island lowlands by 1986–88.
  Native to sub-Saharan Africa; also called St. Helena Waxbill; a popular cage
  bird. (Bishop Museum RLP monograph COMW.pdf; birdsofhawaii.info; Hawai‘i
  Birding Trails; eBird.)
- **Field mark.** Bright red stripe through the eye (the "bandit mask"); finely
  barred gray-brown body; small. (Wikipedia; field guides.)
- **"Most common bird" — CORRECTED.** The waxbill is NOT the world's most
  common bird. Most abundant *wild* bird = **red-billed quelea** (*Quelea
  quelea*, ~1.5 billion breeding birds, sub-Saharan Africa). Most widely
  *distributed* wild bird = **house sparrow** (this book's ch. 5). The waxbill
  is one of the most widely *introduced* estrildid finches — likely the source
  of the "most common/widespread" impression, and easy to conflate with the
  quelea (both small African flocking seed-eaters). (Audubon; BirdNote;
  Bird Watcher's General Store; Bird Spot.)

## Kim-observed anchors [V: Kim]
- **Third watched-invasion.** "Burst on the scene" in Kim's haunts recently;
  the third species he has watched arrive (saffron finch, RCC, waxbill). May
  have missed it earlier because it is tiny.
- **Stalk-bending forage.** Lands partway up a tall seed stalk; its slight
  weight bows the stalk over, lowering the seedhead within reach. (A documented
  estrildid grass-seed behavior; here as Kim's first-hand observation.)
- **Leapfrog flock.** Large flocks; the flock moves in little hops, rear birds
  flitting over front birds and dropping ahead — a rolling leapfrog across the
  lawn.
- **Skittish.** Won't allow approach; the flock lifts as one to a nearby tree
  if pressed. Deliberate contrast with the bold red-crested cardinal.
- **"Worried they'd disappeared."** Expecting the (mis-remembered) "most common
  bird" to be everywhere, Kim feared they had vanished when parks turned up
  none. The correction resolves it: a patchy locally-common finch is exactly
  what it is — present here, absent there. Emotional payoff of the chapter's
  close.
