## Sources (literature-confirmed claims)
- **Introduction before 1855; brought as food birds.** Birds of the nominate
  *S. c. chinensis* (from China/Canton) reached Hawai‘i prior to 1855, probably
  as captives raised for food by Chinese immigrants; rapidly common and
  widespread on all main islands. Native to southern Asia (Afghanistan/India
  through Indochina, SE China, Malay Peninsula, etc.). One of the oldest birds
  in the book — only the rock dove (~1796) predates it; older than the mynah
  (1865). Names: lace-necked dove, Chinese dove. (Birds of the World; Bishop
  Museum RLP monograph SPDO.pdf; birdsofhawaii.info; Wikipedia.)
- **Field marks / ID vs zebra dove.** Larger and browner than the zebra dove;
  broad black neck patch spangled with white spots (the "lace" collar) — vs the
  zebra's fine black barring. Spots vs bars, large vs small = the dove ID pair.
  (Field guides; Wikipedia.)
- **Behavior.** Less gregarious than the zebra dove; usually alone or in pairs.
  (BotW; earthlife.net.) [Kim's "sometimes stands among the seed-eaters as if
  guarding them" is his observation — framed as impression, not claimed
  sentinel behavior.]
- **Bird vision (grounds the close).** Birds are tetrachromatic (4 cone types
  vs human 3), see into the UV, and have cone oil-droplet filters that sharpen
  color discrimination; UV/plumage patterns are used for species and mate
  recognition — i.e., markings humans can't easily resolve are meaningful
  signals to birds. (Cuthill UV-vision proceedings; BirdWatching; avianbliss;
  coblind.) Directly supports Kim's "feather details unseen by people must be
  important to a fellow bird."
