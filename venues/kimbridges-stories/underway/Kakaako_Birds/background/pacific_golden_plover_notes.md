## Sources (literature-confirmed claims)
- **Native migratory.** *Pluvialis fulva*, kōlea (name imitates the flight
  call). Breeds summer in Alaska/Siberia; winters in Hawai‘i (Aug–Apr). NOT
  endemic, NOT introduced — indigenous migratory. (DLNR Kōlea; Hawai‘i Birding
  Trails; Honolulu Zoo Society.)
- **Winter territoriality.** ~60% of individuals defend small feeding
  territories (0.05–0.5 ha) on lawns/open areas, Aug–Apr; stand-scan-dash-peck
  plover foraging. (Honolulu Zoo Society; DLNR SWAP factsheet; Wikipedia.)
- **Site fidelity / return.** Returns to the SAME lawn yearly, ~80% fidelity;
  one banded bird returned to Bellows (windward O‘ahu) 21 years. (Honolulu Zoo
  Society; the long-running O‘ahu colour-band study — Oscar "Wally" Johnson —
  underlies these figures.) Spring: gather at flocking sites late April, then
  **nonstop ~3,000+ mi, 3–4 day** flight to Alaska. (mybirdingjourney; DLNR.)
- **Modern naming/befriending.** Site fidelity → people name and grow attached
  to "their" returning kōlea. (Honolulu Zoo Society; Manu Minute; popular press.)

## Cultural material — HANDLE WITH CARE (verified)
- **ʻŌlelo noʻeau:** *"ʻAi nō ke kōlea a momona a hoʻi i Kahiki"* — "the kōlea
  eats until fat, then returns to Kahiki (the far land)." Said of a person who
  comes, takes the land's bounty, prospers, and leaves without giving back. The
  name *kōlea* is linked to the sense of "one who takes and leaves." Documented
  in Mary Kawena Pukui, *ʻŌlelo Noʻeau* (Bishop Museum Press, 1983). (HPR Manu
  Minute; Waikoloa blog citing Pukui; hokulea.com proverbs.)
  - Framing rule used in the draft: present the proverb as a *metaphor applied
    to people*, giving the bird an ambivalent cultural cast — NOT a flat "the
    Hawaiian people dislike this bird." This is more accurate and respectful;
    Kim's "I've heard it said…" is rendered as the proverb-based ambivalence.
- **Polynesian navigation / wayfinding.** Seasonal kōlea migrations were among
  the signs used by Polynesian navigators; *some scholars believe* the first
  voyagers may have found Hawai‘i by following the spring migration north.
  Present as a scholarly/traditional idea (hedged: "some scholars believe / it
  is thought"), NOT settled fact. (Hawaiian Public Radio; Hana Hou "Flight of
  the Navigators"; popular & scholarly voyaging literature.)
