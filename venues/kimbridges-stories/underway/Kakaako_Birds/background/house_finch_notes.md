## Sources (literature-confirmed claims)
- **Introduction ~1870; on all main islands.** Common to abundant on
  ranchlands / drier habitats by the 1880s–1900s. Native to western North
  America. (Bishop Museum RLP monograph HOFI.pdf; To-Hawaii.com;
  birdsofhawaii.info; HI Audubon.)
- **Local names.** Papaya bird; linnet (borrowed from the European linnet for
  the male's red); crimson-headed finch; **manu ʻai mīkana** ("bird that eats
  papaya"). So this species DOES carry a Hawaiian name (contrast the saffron
  finch's possible lack). (Grokipedia/Avibase; To-Hawaii.com.)
- **Field marks vs House Sparrow** (the teaching core):
  - **Male:** House Finch red on face/breast/rump (varies orange/yellow by
    diet); House Sparrow gray cap + black bib + whitish cheek.
  - **Female (the key):** House Finch **streaked** below (blurry brown streaks
    on a pale breast/flanks); House Sparrow **plain/unstreaked** below, grayish,
    pale supercilium. → *streaked = finch, plain = sparrow* settles most birds.
  - **Bill/structure:** finch bigger thicker (grayish) bill, perches upright
    tail-down; sparrow stouter conical bill, bolder face pattern, longer
    pinkish legs.
  - **Food/habitat:** finch takes fruit/buds (papaya!) and lawn seed, up in
    trees/fruit; sparrow tied more to buildings/human scraps. Both share lawns.
  (Cornell Celebrate Urban Birds; Sibley/BirdWatching; wild-bird-watching;
  Mass Audubon "commonly confused.")

## Kim's framing (the chapter's theme) [V: Kim]
The house finch's problem is identification: a name like the house sparrow's,
a similar look, the same lawn and seeds. Distinguishing them takes skill, and
learning that skill "can turn you into an honest to goodness birder." The keys
are field marks, habitat differences, and food sources — and this is where
field guides, especially LOCAL ones, prove their worth.
