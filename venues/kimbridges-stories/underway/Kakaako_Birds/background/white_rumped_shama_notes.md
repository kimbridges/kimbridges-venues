## Sources (literature-confirmed claims)
- **Introduced O‘ahu 1940 by the HUI MANU SOCIETY** (Kaua‘i 1931 from Malaysia,
  by Alexander Isenberg). Native to South/Southeast Asia (India → Malay
  Peninsula, Java, Borneo). Now common/easily observed on O‘ahu and Kaua‘i;
  spread naturally to Ni‘ihau, Moloka‘i, Lāna‘i. (Bishop Museum RLP monograph
  WRSH.pdf; Birds of the World; birdsofhawaii.info; Wikipedia.)
  → **SECOND Hui Manu species in the book** (with the red-crested cardinal).
- **The song.** One of the most admired songbird voices in Asia: loud, clear,
  varied flutelike whistles; does NOT repeat phrases; rich mimicry of other
  birds woven in; carries several hundred meters. (BotW Sounds; Wikipedia;
  Cornell All About Birds.) Matches Kim's "varied, not simple, loud, clear,
  unexpected."
- **Habitat / "more heard than seen."** Prefers dense undergrowth / forest
  understory; insectivore (gleans + hawks insects); males perch and sing to
  mark territory. The dense-shade requirement explains its rarity in the open
  coastal corridor and its tie to a big shady tree. (Wikipedia; Birding Depot;
  Hawaii Bird Guide.)
- **Note on "rare."** Common on O‘ahu generally (shadier areas), but UNCOMMON in
  Kim's open Kaka‘ako/Ala Moana/downtown corridor specifically, where dense
  understory is scarce — hence tied to the few deep-shade pockets (the banyan).
  Framed honestly in the draft (rare *here*, not rare island-wide).

## Thematic notes
- **Hui Manu thread — COMPLETED.** The cardinal = color; the shama = song. The
  two together embody the Hui Manu's stated purpose ("colorful and/or melodious
  songbirds"). A clean payoff for the recurring thread; worth noting in the
  front matter that the society's two motives are each given a chapter.
- **The "singers" cluster.** Joins the talking mynah and the merbuk zebra dove
  as the book's song birds — and is the supreme voice of the three (brought for
  song alone). In the reorder, place near the mynah.
- **Mutual-regard close** rhymes with the red-crested cardinal ("the bird that
  looks back") — and the curiosity ties to the book's learn-to-see/listen
  thesis. As the last of the "missing birds," its close gestures back at the
  whole book's ask (listen, follow, and be met).
- **In-area anchor:** the Kamehameha-statue banyan sits in the downtown
  government/church district — the eastern edge of the book's defined corridor.
  Good geographic spread (most chapters lean Ala Moana/Kaka‘ako; this one
  anchors the downtown end).
