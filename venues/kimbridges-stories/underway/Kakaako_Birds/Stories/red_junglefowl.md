# Red Junglefowl

*Gallus gallus* · moa · brought by the first voyagers, roughly a thousand years ago

We all call it a chicken. The word is not wrong, but it's misleading, because
it buries the thing that matters most about this bird: of every species in
this book, the so-called chicken has the deepest roots in these islands. And
it was here the better part of a thousand years before many of the others.

## A name older than all the rest

Begin with the isolation. These islands sit farther from any continent than
almost any land on Earth. Now imagine preparing to sail toward them across open
ocean. Toward a place you only believe is out there, with no certainty of
landfall and no way back. What would you carry? Food enough for the crossing,
of course. But if you meant to stay, you would carry something more: living
animals to breed, founding stock to feed the generations that would come after
you in a homeland you had not yet laid eyes on.

That is exactly what the first discoverers of Hawai‘i did. The Polynesian
voyagers who found these islands brought, on the founding canoes, the wild red
junglefowl of Southeast Asia — the ancestor of every chicken alive — along with
the pigs and the taro and the rest of the bundle of canoe plants and animals. This
is what turns an island into a familiar home. [V: lit] They carried it for its meat,
its eggs, its feathers, and its place in ceremony. [V: lit] And they had a name
for it. They called it **moa**, and a thousand years on, people here still call it
that. [V: lit]

## What the word "chicken" hides

Say *chicken* and you summon a barnyard, a coop, a leftover gone wild. Say
*moa* and you summon a voyage — one of the great feats of human navigation,
crossing the largest ocean on the planet in an open canoe. [V: lit] This bird
is not farmyard background. It's a deliberate provision of that crossing, the
founding livestock of a founding people, carried into the unknown so that a
homeland not yet seen would have something to eat. To learn what the chicken
really is, then, is to learn something about the people who brought it. This
is the quiet argument of this whole book. The most familiar bird of all turns
out to hold the oldest human story of this place.

## The bird in its genes

Look closely and the living bird is a record with two layers. The feral
chickens crossing the lawns and parking lots today are a genetic blend: the
ancient moa underneath, overlaid with ordinary modern domestic chickens that
escaped within living memory. Many domestic chickens were set loose when Hurricanes Iwa in
1982 and Iniki in 1992 tore the coops apart across the islands. A 2022 genetic
study reads both layers in the same birds. [V: lit] So the rooster strutting
across the asphalt carries two histories at once in a single body: a voyage a
thousand years ago, and a single stormy night of escape. The deep past and the
recent accident, braided together in one ordinary bird.

## The first immigrants' bird

Every other introduced bird in this book is a piece of some later homeland: a
Brazilian cardinal, an African waxbill, an Asian bulbul. Each carried here in
the last two centuries by people who came holding a fragment of where they were
from. The moa is the first of them all, and the deepest. It is the bird of the
first immigrants: the people who found these islands and stayed, who carried
their food-bird across an ocean into the unknown so that life could continue on
the far side of the world. [V: Kim] [V: lit] We call it a chicken and step
around it in the road. It's the oldest tie, in feathers, to the first human
story of this place.
