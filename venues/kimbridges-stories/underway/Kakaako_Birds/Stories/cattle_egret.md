# Cattle Egret

*Bubulcus ibis* · introduced to O‘ahu, 1959

A bird that was brought here to do a job, did it, and then quit to do
something far more interesting.

## A solution that became a problem

In the summer of 1959 the Territory's Board of Agriculture and Forestry,
backed by cattle ranchers and the Hui Manu Society, shipped in 105 young
cattle egrets from southern Florida. [V: lit] The idea was tidy: the
birds would ride with the herds, eat the flies and ticks that cost
ranchers hide quality and weight gain, and earn their keep as living
pest control. [V: lit] It worked. It also did not stop there. Within two
decades the statewide population had climbed past 30,000, and the bird
that had been imported as a remedy was being recategorized as a pest in
its own right. [V: lit]

That arc — from imported solution to naturalized problem — is the frame
for everything else about this bird in Kaka‘ako. What makes it worth a
chapter is not that the introduction went wrong, but *why* it went so
thoroughly right for the egret.

## Built to follow a mower it had never seen

The cattle egret did not evolve alongside cattle. It evolved in Africa,
following the megafauna — the buffalo, the elephants, the antelope —
walking just ahead of those great moving feet and snatching the
grasshoppers and other insects the herd flushed out of the grass. [V: lit]
The relationship is commensal and efficient: a cattle egret foraging
beside a large mover catches several times more prey than one hunting
alone. [V: lit] When tractors and mowers replaced animals on the world's
pastures, the egret made the substitution without missing a step. A
machine that stirs insects out of the grass is, to this bird, simply a
slow metal buffalo. [V: lit]

You can watch the whole evolutionary argument play out on a weekday
morning at Kaka‘ako Waterfront Park. When the landscape crews bring out
the mowers, a loose herd of egrets forms up behind the blades and walks
the cut line, stabbing at whatever the machine throws up. [V: Kim] They
show no fear of the engine noise. They are not tolerating the mower;
they are *using* it, exactly as their ancestors used the buffalo.

## The opportunist's second act

The same flexibility that let the egret adopt the mower let it adopt the
prawn pond. When commercial freshwater prawn farming opened at Kahuku,
the egrets found the shallow, densely stocked ponds easier hunting than
any pasture, and poaching prawns became a recognized economic problem
for the farms almost from the day they opened. [V: lit] [V: Kim — present
at the ponds' dedication] A bird imported to *help* one kind of
agriculture had, within a generation, become a cost line for another.

## What it costs the natives

The cattle egret's success is not free. It is now listed as invasive in
Hawai‘i, and beyond the prawn losses it competes with — and preys on the
nests of — native waterbirds including the koloa, the ae‘o (Hawaiian
stilt), and the ‘alae ke‘oke‘o (Hawaiian coot). [V: lit] An elegant white
bird on a green lawn is a pleasant thing to photograph; it is also a
reminder that "useful" and "harmless" are not the same word.

## Field notes

In breeding season the adult grows orange-buff plumes across the crown,
back, and breast, and the bill flushes orange to red; the rest of the
year it is plain white. [V: lit] Immature birds carry a black bill. [V: lit]
