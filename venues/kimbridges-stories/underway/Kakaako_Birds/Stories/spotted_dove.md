# Spotted Dove

*Spilopelia chinensis* · the Lace-necked Dove · introduced to Hawai‘i, before 1855

The larger of Honolulu's two everyday doves keeps mostly to itself. And
wears, around its neck, the single most beautiful piece of detail in this
whole book, which almost no one has ever actually seen.

## An old arrival, brought for the pot

The spotted dove has been here a very long time. It reached the islands
before 1855. It came most likely as captive birds raised for food by Chinese
immigrants. That makes it one of the oldest birds in these pages; only the
pigeon got here ahead of it, and it predates even the mynah. [V: lit] A native
of southern Asia in its Chinese form, it spread quickly and is now common on
every main island. [V: lit] Its names carry its whole story: the *Chinese
dove* for where it came from, the *lace-necked dove* for the thing it wears.
And its way in was its own. Not pest control, not the cage-fancy trade, but
the cooking pot.

## The loner that keeps company

Where the zebra dove of the last chapter gathers in busy little crowds, the
spotted dove is the bigger, quieter bird that you usually meet alone or in a
single pair. [V: Kim] [V: lit] You will know it at once by the contrast:
larger than the zebra dove, browner, and wearing across the back of the neck a
broad black patch spangled with white spots instead of the zebra's fine bars.
Spots, not bars; large, not small. The pair is easy once you have both. And
yet, now and then, this loner does something unexpected: it will settle in
among a group of the smaller seed-eaters and simply stand there with them, a
calm, larger presence at the edge of the flock, almost as if it had appointed
itself their guard. [V: Kim]

## The spots you cannot see

The neck patch is the thing to stay with. From a few feet away the "lace"
looks like a simple scatter of pale dots on black — pretty, but plain. Put a
close-up lens on it, though, and each single spot resolves into something
intricately built, a small structured architecture of feather far more complex
than the unaided eye ever reports. [V: Kim] I have these in rare photographs,
and they still surprise me. You do not see this standing in the park; only the
camera sees it. And that is the quiet lesson of the spotted dove: there is an
enormous amount of detail on a bird that a person simply cannot resolve. [V: Kim]

## What the bird sees

Which raises the real question. If we cannot see it, who is it for? Not us.
Birds are built to see far more than we do. They are tetrachromats, carrying a
fourth color channel that reaches into the ultraviolet, with oil-droplet
filters in the eye that split apart colors that we read as a single shade. 
Markings
that are faint or invisible to a person can be vivid and exact to a bird. And
they are used in earnest; to tell one species from another, and one mate from
the next. [V: lit] The spotted dove's necklace, which we need a macro lens
even to glimpse, is plain speech to another dove. Much of this book has been an argument for
learning to see the common birds around you. Here the lesson turns around:
even once you have learned to see them, they see a far richer world than you
ever will, and there is always more on a bird than the eye is given to find. [V: Kim] [V: lit]
