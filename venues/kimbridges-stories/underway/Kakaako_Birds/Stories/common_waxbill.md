# Common Waxbill

*Estrilda astrild* · introduced to O‘ahu, 1970s

A bird that's small enough to overlook entirely — until you catch the red mask
across its eyes, worn like a cartoon bandit's bandana. After that you will
never mistake it for anything else.

## A latecomer from Africa

The common waxbill is one of the newest arrivals in this book, and one I
watched turn up in person. A small finch of sub-Saharan Africa and a long-
popular cage bird, it established itself on O‘ahu in the 1970s from escaped
cage birds. A flock was first noted in a cane field near ‘Ewa Beach in 1973 
and within about fifteen years it had spread through most of the island's
lowlands. [V: lit] It's the second bird in these pages to arrive that way,
by the same back door as the saffron finch: a cage bird that let itself out.
For me it simply appeared one day in the places I visit, the third species I
have watched move into Honolulu. [V: Kim] It's so tiny that I may well have
walked past it many times before I ever registered it.

## The bandit at the grass stalk

The mask is the thing to learn: a bright red stripe straight through the eye,
exactly a small thief's bandana, on an otherwise finely barred gray-brown
bird. [V: lit] Once you see this, the bird is unmistakable. Watch a waxbill
feed and you get a small lesson in mechanics: it will fly up and land partway
along a tall seed stalk, and its slight weight is just enough to bow the stalk
gently over, lowering the ripe seedhead to where the bird can strip it. [V: Kim]
The bird is its own tool for bending the harvest down to itself.

## A flock that leapfrogs

Waxbills go about in flocks, sometimes large ones, and a feeding flock moves
in a way worth stopping for. Rather than walking steadily, the whole group
travels in little hops. The birds at the back flitter over the ones in front
and drop down ahead of them, so the flock rolls forward across a lawn in a
continuous leapfrog. [V: Kim] They are wary, too, and here they part company
with the bold red-crested cardinal: a waxbill will not let you approach. Press
toward them and the flock lifts as one to a nearby tree and waits you out.
[V: Kim]

## Not the most common bird in the world

There is a thing often said about this bird — I had read it myself — that the
common waxbill is the most common bird species on Earth. It is not. That
distinction belongs to another small African seed-eater it's easy to confuse
it with, the red-billed quelea. That bird has a breeding population of something
like a billion and a half. But the waxbill is one of the most widely
*introduced* finches. Why? It was carried around the world by the cage trade. [V: lit]
(The widest-traveled of all, as it happens, is the house sparrow, met
earlier.) [V: lit]

The correction is worth making because it lays a small worry to rest. Believing
I was watching the world's commonest bird, I expected, after a few years, to
find waxbills absolutely everywhere. And when a park visit turned up none, I
began to fear they had gone. They had not. A locally common, patchy introduced
finch is precisely what the waxbill is: here on this lawn, absent from that
one, never promised at any particular place. [V: Kim] [V: lit] They are still
about, and the pleasure of them is exactly that they are not guaranteed.
