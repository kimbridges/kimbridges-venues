# Ruddy Turnstone

*Arenaria interpres* · ‘akekeke · a native migratory visitor

On one of the busiest strip of sand in Honolulu, among the towels and the sunscreen,
a few small calico birds work the rocky edges almost unnoticed. They are the
easiest birds in this book to walk right past. And, mile for mile, the
greatest travelers in it.

## Hiding on a crowded beach

You have to be paying attention to see them at all. I have only ever found a
small group — perhaps five birds at a time — and in the most unlikely setting:
the heavily used sand of Ala Moana Beach Park. [V: Kim] There, among the rocky
coral fragments at the water's edge, they poke and probe for aquatic food,
always on the move. Now and then they retreat up onto the open sand, or even
in among the concrete and lawns of the human shoreline. [V: Kim] But make no
mistake, these are true shoreline birds. Their dull, mottled winter plumage
is a kind of calico camouflage that breaks them up against the coral and lets
them vanish in plain sight. [V: lit] Lie out for a tan if you like; just keep
one eye open, and you may be lucky enough to catch these elusive little
shoreline critters at work. [V: Kim]

## The bird that turns the stones

Watch one feed and its name explains itself. The turnstone makes its living by
overturning the shoreline. It uses its strong neck and a short, slightly wedge-
shaped bill to flip aside stones, shells, and clumps of seaweed and snatch the
small invertebrates hiding underneath. [V: lit] That habit is the English name
outright: *turnstone*. The Hawaiian name comes from another direction
entirely — *‘akekeke*, after the bird's dry rattling call, *a-ke-ke-ke*.
[V: lit] Special slightly spiny feet help it grip the wet rock while it works.
It's a small bird that earns its keep by lifting the lids off the edge of the
sea.

## A traveler in disguise

Here is what the sunbathers stepping around it never suspect. This
unremarkable beach bird is a native. But a native that is only visiting, and
that arrives under its own astonishing power. The ruddy turnstone is one of the
most northerly-nesting birds on Earth: it breeds on high-Arctic tundra and
crosses thousands of miles of open ocean to winter on these shores. It's here from
about August until it leaves for the Arctic again in spring. [V: lit] Some make
the run from Alaska to Hawai‘i in long overwater hauls of more than a thousand
kilometers a day. [V: lit] And the most staggering part is how the young ones
arrive. A juvenile turnstone takes its first flight at under three weeks old. 
And within a few days, it launches out over the open Pacific toward wintering
grounds it has never seen. Its parents have already gone on ahead, leaving it to
navigate thousands of miles entirely alone. [V: lit] The little birds among the
coral at Ala Moana did exactly that to reach this beach.

## A different kind of native

Set this bird against the rest of the book and the contrast is the whole point.
No one carried the turnstone here. It was not brought for food or song or color
or by accident; it comes on its own wings, as its ancestors have come far
longer than there have been people on these shores. This is an annual thread stitched
between the top of the world and these islands, older than the first canoe.
[V: lit] It is native not by staying but by returning. Look down, next time, at
that crowded ribbon of sand: the calico bird flipping a chip of coral is a
traveler from the Arctic who found this exact beach without a map, and will
find the tundra again come spring. [V: Kim] [V: lit]
