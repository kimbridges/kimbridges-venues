# Saffron Finch

*Sicalis flaveola* · introduced from South America, around 1965

If the mynah and the egret are old residents, the saffron finch is the
new neighbor. A small, vivid yellow bird whose arrival in Honolulu is
recent enough that you could have watched it happen.

## The bird that let itself in

The first two birds in this book came by deliberate public programs. The
mynah was brought in 1865 to fight insect pests; the cattle egret in 1959
as living fly control. The saffron finch came by a third route — the
private cage-bird trade. It is a popular aviary bird in its native South
America, kept for its color and its song, and it established itself in
Hawai‘i around 1965 from cage birds that escaped or were deliberately
turned loose. [V: lit]

Much of that release can be traced to a single place. The ranchland at Pu‘u Wa‘a
Wa‘a on the Big Island was, through the mid-1900s, a kind of ground zero
for setting exotic finches and waxbills free. Lavender waxbills,
cordon-bleus, seedeaters, weavers; released birds by the cageful.
[V: lit] Most of those species flared and vanished. The saffron finch is
one of the few that caught. So the natural question — why was a tropical
South American finch sitting in a cage on a Hawaiian ranch at all? — has a
tidy answer: an era of amateur aviculture put it there, and the open
island did the rest. The casual bird-keeping that seeded it is now the
very thing the state regulates against; importing a pet bird to Hawai‘i
today takes permits and quarantine, precisely because of stories like this
one. [V: lit]

## A colonization you can watch

On the Big Island the saffron finch settled in quickly and is now common
across the lowlands. [V: lit] O‘ahu has been slower, and that slowness is
what makes the bird interesting: its spread here has unfolded within
living memory. Birds turned up sporadically around Honolulu in the 1990s;
through the 2000s they appeared in Waipahu, Mililani, ‘Ewa Beach, and
Nānākuli; by the early 2010s they had reached Nu‘uanu Valley and the
North Shore toward Hale‘iwa. [V: lit] Today they are locally common on
O‘ahu, favoring exactly the kind of open, short-grass coastal ground that
Kaka‘ako's lawns provide. [V: lit]

I can date the bird's absence precisely. In 2002 I drew up a list of birds
for University of Hawai‘i students to find as a laboratory exercise, and I
was watching the urban birds closely in those years — campus and city
both. The saffron finch was not on the list, because it was not yet here to
see; had it been around, I would have found it. [V: Kim] A few years later
I did spot one, but it was still too scarce to photograph. I have been on
the hunt ever since, and the hunt has gotten easier: now I can count on
seeing them at Kaka‘ako Waterfront Park, and sometimes at Ala Moana Beach
Park. [V: Kim] It's not the only bird I have watched cross from
first-sighting to commonplace. But it is one of the most recent, and one
of the most satisfying to have caught in the act. Most introduced birds in
this book are finished stories; the saffron finch is one whose map is still
being drawn.

## Knowing one when you see it

The male is the easy one: bright yellow with a distinct orange wash across
the crown that separates him from the other yellowish finches around
town. [V: lit] Females and males look broadly alike, though females are
duller and more olive-brown, and juveniles are buffier still — gray-brown
with only a hint of yellow. [V: lit] On a green Kaka‘ako lawn the adult
male is hard to miss; the rest of the family takes a second look.
