# Rock Dove

*Columba livia* · the Pigeon · introduced to Hawai‘i, around 1796

This is the bird the book exists to test. It is easy to admire a saffron
finch; it takes a little discipline to give the pigeon the same honest
look. But a fair field guide does not grade on charm, so here is the
pigeon, looked at squarely.

## The oldest immigrant

For all the disdain it collects, the rock dove was here first. It reached
Hawai‘i around 1796 — earlier than the mynah, the egret, or the saffron
finch. Arriving not as wildlife but as livestock, the feral descendants
of domestic pigeons kept for food, for message-carrying, and for show.
[V: lit] Its wild ancestors were cliff birds of Europe, Africa, and Asia,
nesting on rocky sea ledges and desert escarpments. [V: lit] That ancestry
is the key to everything people hold against it. A modern city, to a rock
dove, is not a degraded place. It's an excellent imitation of a sea
cliff: sheer faces, sheltered ledges, updrafts, and crevices for nesting.
The pigeon downtown is not slumming. It's precisely, expertly at home. The
bird most people read as a symbol of urban decay is in fact one of the
best-adapted animals in the city.

## A flock is a mirror

Watch where the pigeons gather — a bus stop, a plaza, a fast-food
forecourt — and you are really watching a map of human habit. The birds
collect where people gather because some of those people feed them, day
after day, until the spot becomes a fixture on the flock's daily round.
[V: Kim] The degradation that follows — the fouled pavement, the litter of
crumbs and droppings, the general dinginess of a heavily used corner — is
a joint production of human traffic and bird activity, and it is easy to
blame entirely on the bird. [V: Kim] But the pigeon did not choose the bus
stop. We did, every time someone opened a bag of bread there. The flock is
less a nuisance the bird commits than a habit we taught it.

The proof is in the birds that no one is feeding. Away from the handouts,
a rock dove behaves like any other seed-eater. Watch it working quietly across a
lawn for grass seed, unhurried, not scavenging a heap of trash. [V: Kim]
Observe those lawn birds for a while and the disfavor lifts; they are simply
doing what birds do. The trash-pile pigeon and the lawn pigeon are the same
species behaving in two settings, and it's the feeders who manufacture the
first one. And, with it, the reputation the whole species carries. The
people most responsible for the bird's bad name are precisely the ones who
think they are being kind to it. [V: Kim]

## Feeding, and the law

Hawai‘i has tried to legislate the habit. Excessive feeding of feral birds
is a public-health nuisance under state law. This is defined, concretely, as
feeding in a way a reasonable person would expect to draw twenty-five or
more feral birds to linger within a twenty-foot radius. There is a fine for a
first offense and a petty misdemeanor for a repeat. [V: lit] The law exempts
permitted racing and show pigeons, the bird's respectable relations. [V: lit]
In practice enforcement is thin. By some accounts, no citations have been
issued at all. And the feeding goes on, with the pigeon as its usual
beneficiary. [V: lit] [V: Kim] The bird breaks no law; only the feeder does,
and the feeder is rarely stopped.

## The flock that was white

The pigeon's colors are not even fixed across a lifetime of watching. In
the 1960s the flocks I knew were pure white. Every bird. And a
white flock wheeling together was something to behold. [V: Kim] Over the
years that followed, the ordinary colors arrived, and the white gave way to
the mottled mix you see today. I was sorry to watch it happen; for a while
the early mixtures were less lovely than the white had been, and I suspect
the "motley" look the flock eventually took on has added to the species'
disfavor. [V: Kim] Now a few interesting colors abound, though some are
easier on the eye than others.

There is real biology under that change, and it is not settled science. A
white flock points back to a founding stock of white domestic birds. As
other escapees joined and the gene pool mixed, the colors multiplied.
[V: lit] What's striking is that the mixing did not simply wash back to
the wild ancestor. Geneticists once expected feral flocks to drift toward
the original blue-barred plumage over the generations. There is real
pressure that way, since blue-bar birds are caught by peregrine falcons
less often than pale ones. Yet city flocks stay stubbornly varied, and
exactly why is still debated. [V: lit] The shifting palette of a pigeon
flock is a live question in evolutionary biology, not just street scenery.

## Worth a second look

There is a reward for the discipline of looking. Because feral pigeons
descend from centuries of domestic breeds, no two birds in a flock are
quite alike. Blue-bars, checkers, rusty reds, pieds, and the descendants
of those old whites, all mixed together where a truly wild species would
look uniform. [V: lit] The iridescence on a gray neck, in the right light,
is as fine as anything on a more celebrated bird. Learning to see the
pigeon — really see it — is part of learning to see at all. That, after
all, is the whole point.
