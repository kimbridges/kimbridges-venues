# Hawaiian Duck

*Anas wyvilliana* · koloa maoli · endemic; endangered

Linger at the canal long enough and a duck or two will paddle past. A duck —
and if you know your mainland birds, you think *mallard*, and you look away.
That glance, it turns out, is the whole tragedy of this bird in miniature.

## A duck that isn't a mallard

It does look like a mallard. Specifically like a female mallard, mottled brown
all over. But this is the koloa, Hawai‘i's own duck, an endemic species found
nowhere else on Earth. [V: lit] A pure koloa is darker than a mallard and a
fifth to a third smaller, and its full Hawaiian name, *koloa maoli*, means the
"true" or "native" duck. It's a name that, as we will see, is doing painful work.
[V: lit] It is federally endangered, and it is hiding, in plain sight, behind
the most familiar duck-face in the world.

## Nowhere much to be a duck

There is not a lot of room to be a duck in this part of town. The koloa is not
an ocean bird; you will not find it out in the surf. [V: Kim] What it needs is
fresh, calm water, and in the Kaka‘ako region that comes only in a few mostly
man-made pockets: the pond that wraps the State Capitol has drawn ducks, and
there are a few spots down by the Zoo, but it's the canal in our stretch that
is the real magnet. [V: Kim] This is not a big area, but it's home to a few
birds. [V: Kim] An endemic duck of the most isolated archipelago on Earth,
reduced in the city to a handful of engineered ponds and a tidal ditch that is
only sometimes fresh enough to suit it. [V: lit]

## Two threats, one of them quiet

The shortage of habitat is one pressure on the koloa. There are others: 
introduced cats, rats, and mongooses take eggs and ducklings; and people have
always had a use for a duck. Nearly every major cuisine makes a place for one.
[V: lit] [V: Kim] But the gravest threat is the one almost no one would guess,
and it is genetic. The koloa freely interbreeds with feral mallards, and the
hybrids are steadily swallowing the native gene pool. This is a slow genetic dilution
that biologists describe, without exaggeration, as genetic extinction. [V: lit]
On O‘ahu it is already far advanced: most of the island's koloa-looking ducks
are now koloa–mallard hybrids, and genetically pure koloa survive mainly on
Kaua‘i. [V: lit]

## The cruelest resemblance

So return to that first careless glance. The very likeness that lets a visitor
file the koloa away as a mallard is the same likeness that lets the two birds
interbreed. The koloa is being lost not by being shot or starved but by being
*blended away*. Each generation becomes a little more mallard, a little less koloa,
until the endemic persists only as a fraction inside a mixed bird. [V: lit]
There is no villain in this, which is what makes it the hardest kind of loss to
tell: someone, somewhere, let a few mallards loose on ordinary-looking
water. And a bird found nowhere else on Earth began dissolving into its own
look-alike. Conservation managers fight it the only way they can. They remove
feral mallards and hybrids, guard the pure birds, and protect what wetland is
left. But it is slow, sorrowful work. [V: lit] Look again, then, and look
closely, at the pair paddling the canal. If they are still koloa — truly koloa
— they are among the rarest birds in this entire book, and they are watching
their own kind vanish into a resemblance. [V: Kim] [V: lit]
