# Pacific Golden-Plover

*Pluvialis fulva* · kōlea · a native migratory visitor

The turnstone of the last chapter roves the shoreline in a restless little
band. The kōlea is its opposite in nearly every way: it claims one patch of
open lawn, holds it alone, and rules it. The same patch, year after year. Of
all the birds in this book, it may be the one that has been in the longest and
deepest conversation with the people who live here.

## King of the lawn

Watch a kōlea and you watch a monarch survey its grounds. It stands tall and
often nearly motionless, scanning the grass; then it dashes a few steps, stabs
down for an insect working through the turf, and resumes its still, upright
watch. [V: Kim] This is not idle loitering. It's a feeding territory, a
patch of a fraction of an acre that the bird defends through the whole winter,
August to April. [V: lit] I like to call them **lawn sharks**, and the name fits:
a top predator cruising its own stretch of green, picking off the unwary, a
functional echo of the reef shark on its patch of coral. [V: Kim] And the
hunting must pay, because the bird comes back to the very same lawn the next
year, and the year after that. [V: Kim]

## The bird that comes home

That return is the kōlea's most astonishing trait. It comes back not merely to
Hawai‘i but to the *same lawn*, with about eighty percent fidelity. One famous
banded bird returned to the same spot at Bellows, on windward O‘ahu, for
twenty-one years running. [V: lit] Between visits it flies to Alaska and back. That's
a nonstop run of more than three thousand miles. It takes three or four days
traveling over open ocean. It does this twice a year. [V: lit] 
This faithfulness is why the kōlea, almost alone
among the birds here, becomes a person's friend. Most birds are just "birds."
But a kōlea that claims your yard each August, that you watch grow fat and molt
into its black-and-gold finery and depart each spring, and that you then see
land on the same grass the following fall — that bird is given a name. People grow
genuinely attached to their annual visitor. [V: Kim] [V: lit]

## Fattens, and leaves

And yet the kōlea's relationship with Hawai‘i's host culture is famously mixed,
and the reason is the very leaving that the rest of us find so poignant. There
is an ʻōlelo noʻeau — a traditional saying — *"ʻAi nō ke kōlea a momona a hoʻi
i Kahiki"*: the kōlea eats until it is fat, then returns to Kahiki, the distant
land. [V: lit] This is what is said of a person who comes, takes what the land freely
offers, grows prosperous on it, and then departs without giving back. The
bird's own name carries the sense of one who takes and leaves. [V: lit] So the
same bird that one household names and loves, the tradition casts as the
freeloader who fattens and goes. Friend and opportunist, welcomed and
side-eyed at once. It's exactly the kind of knotted, two-sided relationship
between birds and people that this book keeps turning up. The kōlea does not
let you settle on a single feeling about it. [V: Kim]

## The birds that showed the way

There is a deeper side to the kōlea's leaving, though, and it reaches back to
the very first chapter of human life on these islands. Each spring the flocks
gather and lift off to the north. They go the same direction, on the same schedule,
every year. To a people who read the sea and sky for meaning, that's not loss;
that's information. The seasonal flights of migratory birds were among the signs
Polynesian navigators used in wayfinding, and some scholars believe the first
voyagers may have found Hawai‘i by following the spring migration of birds
northward from the islands of the south. [V: lit] Picture it from a canoe:
flocks streaming off each year toward an empty horizon, always the same way. A
living highway pointing toward land that no one had yet seen. Recall the moa, from near the start of this book, riding in the founding
canoe as food for an unknown homeland. The kōlea may have been the other half of that voyage: not the
provision carried aboard, but the sign in the sky that told the canoe where to
steer.

## Native by return

Like the turnstone, the kōlea is native not by staying but by returning, an
annual thread stitched between the Arctic and these islands. But where the
turnstone is hidden in a flock at the water's edge, the kōlea stands alone and in
the open and makes itself known. It claims its ground, earns a name, carries a
proverb, and may once have drawn the first people across the sea to this place.
[V: Kim] [V: lit] Look for the still bird standing sentinel on the grass, watching
its domain. It will be back next year, on that same spot. It always is.
