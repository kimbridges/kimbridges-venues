# House Finch

*Haemorhous mexicanus* · the Papaya Bird, the Linnet · introduced to Hawai‘i, around 1870

This is the bird that can make you a birder. Not because it is rare or
dramatic. It is neither. But because telling it apart from the house
sparrow is the first real test of the eye, and passing it changes how you
see.

## Two birds, one lawn

The house finch sets a trap that the rest of this book's birds do not. Its
name echoes the house sparrow's; the two are close in size and both are
brownish; and they feed side by side on the same lawns, after the same
seeds. [V: Kim] [V: lit] A beginner glances at the pair, files them both
under "little brown bird," and moves on. That glance is the thing this
chapter is here to break. Learning to split these two on sight is the exact
moment looking becomes birding. And once you can do it, you cannot go back
to not seeing it.

## Reading the marks

The males are a gift to the beginner. A male house finch is washed with red
across the face, breast, and rump — sometimes more orange or even yellow,
depending on what the bird has been eating — while the male house sparrow
wears a gray cap and a black bib at the throat. [V: lit] Red against black:
no real contest there.

The females are the true lesson, because both are plain brown and neither
helps you with color. Here the deciding mark is the breast. The house finch
is **streaked** below — blurry brown streaks running down a pale breast and
flanks, a textured look — while the house sparrow is **clean** below,
unstreaked and grayish, with a pale line swept back from the eye. [V: lit]
Streaked breast, finch; plain breast, sparrow. That one mark settles most
birds you will meet. If you want to confirm it, the finch carries a bigger,
thicker bill and tends to perch upright with the tail angled down; the
sparrow's bill is a stouter cone and its face is the more boldly patterned
of the two. [V: lit]

## Habitat and the papaya

The bird's other names point you to a second kind of clue. *Papaya bird*,
*manu ʻai mīkana* — the bird that eats papaya. [V: lit] The house finch
takes fruit and buds as happily as it takes lawn seed, and you will find it
up in the trees and at the fruit where a house sparrow, tied more closely to
buildings and human scraps, is less likely to be. [V: lit] Where a bird
feeds, and on what, is itself a field mark. A slower one than plumage, but
just as real.

## The field guide, and the making of a birder

This is the point where a good field guide earns its keep. Especially
one written for local birds, which mercifully shrinks the whole confusing
world down to the dozen or so species you will actually meet on a Honolulu
lawn. [V: Kim] Work out this one pair, finch from sparrow, and something
permanent happens. You stop seeing "a little brown bird" and start seeing
two species, told apart on purpose, by marks you can name. That skill is
exactly what a birder has that a passerby does not. And it generalizes: the
next tricky pair falls to the same patience. The house finch, in other
words, is where this book's quiet promise comes true. Learn the common ones,
and the world rearranges itself into things you can tell apart.
