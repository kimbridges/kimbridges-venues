# Black-crowned Night Heron

*Nycticorax nycticorax* · ‘auku‘u · native

You might assume a fish-eater would head for the ocean to feed. Some do;  a
white tern will fly miles out to sea for its meals. The ‘auku‘u found a smarter,
lazier table much closer to home: a canal.

## The urban café

Walk the length of Ala Moana Beach Park, back parallel to Ala Moana Boulevard, and
you'll find a canal of brackish water that rises and falls with the tide.
[V: Kim] At times it runs a foot or so deep; at low tide it draws down, and in
the depressions along its course the water is left behind as shallow ponds. Any
fish that failed to read the falling tide is now trapped in one of those ponds,
with nowhere to go. [V: Kim] This is the night heron's opportunity, and it
works it patiently. The bird perches on the canal's edge and waits; there are
plenty of fish here, so it can afford to. [V: Kim] The tide does the herding;
the heron only has to keep its table.

## A patient ambush

Patience is the whole of the heron's technique. It is a "stand and wait"
hunter. It settles into a hunched, motionless crouch at the water's edge,
stares down into the shallows, and holds utterly still until a fish drifts into
range, then unfolds in a single lunging strike. [V: lit] It's stocky and
short-necked for a heron, with a black crown and back, gray wings, pale
underparts, and a startling red eye. The name in English comes with a harsh,
carrying *quok* of a call. [V: lit] The "night" in the name only half applies
in Hawai‘i: mainland night herons feed mostly after dark, but the birds here
hunt by day as readily as at dusk. That's why you can watch one work the
canal in full afternoon sun. [V: lit]

## Tables along the water

Watch the whole length of the canal and a pattern appears. The herons are
spaced out along it, each to its own stretch, politely separated down the line
of water. [V: Kim] They are territorial feeders, holding exclusive patches. 
But the spacing here is relaxed rather than fierce, because there is enough for
everyone; the canal is long and the fish are many. [V: lit] [V: Kim] There is
something about the formality of the place. This is a straight, uniform, engineered
channel. That makes me think of how a city lays out a row of cafés: inside, diners
are seated at intervals along a counter, each working its own plate, no need to
quarrel when the food keeps coming. This is the ‘auku‘u's urban café, and the
herons are its regular patrons, each at a favorite table. [V: Kim]

## A native at the urban table

Here is the quiet turn. For all that it dines on is in a human-built channel, the
‘auku‘u is **native**. This is Hawai‘i's only native heron, indigenous to these
islands and present here long before there was a canal, a boulevard, or a park
to name it after. [V: lit] And yet it has taken to this concrete, tide-fed
ditch as fluently as any introduced bird ever took to a lawn or a bus stop. It
is a useful reminder that being native is not the same as keeping apart from
us. This native fisher has simply read the city's plumbing as one more kind of
wetland, and set up its table where the water and the fish reliably meet. It
belongs here in the oldest sense, and it has learned the new Honolulu as
quickly as any newcomer.
