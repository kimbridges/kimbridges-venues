# Java Sparrow

*Lonchura oryzivora* · the Java rice bird · introduced to O‘ahu, 1960s

In the brown-and-streaky crowd of small seed-eaters, the Java sparrow is the
one in formal wear. It's the best-dressed bird on the lawn, and the only one
you can never mistake for anything else.

## Unmistakable

The markings settle the identification in a glance: a clean pale-gray body, a
black cap and tail, a crisp white cheek, a bold red ring around the eye, and a
heavy pink-red bill set in the black of the face. [V: Kim] [V: lit] Set that
beside the muddled browns of the sparrows and finches and waxbills it feeds
among, and there is simply no confusing it. The Java sparrow is tailored,
formal, almost dapper. If you scored these birds on looks, this one would
finish near the top. [V: Kim] And that stout red bill is a plain declaration of
the bird's business: it is a serious seed-cracker. Its scientific name,
*oryzivora*, means "rice-eater," and across much of the world it is known
simply as the rice bird. [V: lit]

## A flock at the uncut lawn

You meet it in flocks, and in a particular sort of spot: a patch the mowing
crew has missed, where the grass has been left long enough to go to seed.
[V: Kim] That is where the food is — heads of grass heavy with seed — and that
is where the Java sparrows gather to work it over. It is a bird that profits
from a neglected corner; a missed mowing is its banquet.

## Brought for its beauty

So why is it here at all? For the reason you would guess from looking at it. The
Java sparrow is one of the most popular cage birds on Earth. Its been kept for centuries
for its handsome plumage and its gentle, calm manner. It reached O‘ahu in the
1960s the same way the saffron finch and the waxbill did. It came through the cage
trade, as birds that escaped or were released. [V: lit] Like the red-crested
cardinal, it's here because someone found it beautiful. The same aesthetic
impulse that the Hui Manu Society formalized when it imported songbirds for
their color worked again, less formally, with this one. Beauty was its passport.

## The cost of being beautiful

And that beauty has been a disaster at home. In its native Java and Bali, where
it was once abundant in the rice fields, the Java sparrow has been trapped so
relentlessly for the cage trade — by one survey, more than a third of the
adults taken in a single year — that it is now listed as Endangered. There are fewer
than ten thousand wild birds left and the number still falling. [V: lit] Hold
that fact against the lawn in front of you, where the same species feeds in an
easy, untroubled flock. [V: lit] It's the mirror image of the koloa, the endemic duck of these
islands. The koloa is vanishing at home, here in Hawai‘i; the Java
sparrow is a bird vanishing at *its* home, and flourishing here as an exile.
Both losses run through the same human hand. The one careless, the other
covetous. The Java sparrow carries the sharper paradox of the two: it's
being trapped toward extinction in Java for precisely the beauty that has made
it secure on a patch of grass five thousand miles away. The handsomest bird in
the lawn is a refugee from its own desirability. [V: Kim] [V: lit]
