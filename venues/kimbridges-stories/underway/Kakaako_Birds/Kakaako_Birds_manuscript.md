# The Birds Around Kaka‘ako

*K. W. Bridges with Claude (Anthropic)*

## Preface

Years ago, Dr. Will McClatchey and I set out to rebuild
the laboratory exercises for an introductory biology course. We wanted to give
students real skills they could keep using outside the classroom, a
piece of authentic biology that would outlast the course. 
For one exercise, we chose bird identification. It was a practical choice 
because there were then only sixteen bird species
on the university campus. Sixteen is a number you can teach. With a
handful of photographs and about an hour, all the lab students
could learn to name every bird they were likely to see on campus.

What happened next was the point. Once a person can identify everything they
ordinarily see, they can recognize something new. The unfamiliar bird stands
out because it breaks the pattern the eye has learned and practiced. And that
moment of *that one's different* is very important. It's the very thing a field 
biologist relies on. The entry cost is low and the reward compounds: learn the common set,
and the next bird comes easily, and the one after that, until a person who
started with sixteen knows fifty without trying. It's a skill they keep for life.
This book is built to do the same thing for the birds of one small corner of
Honolulu. It means to show you that there are not too many to learn — and to
tell you enough good stories that you will want to learn more.

What follows are stories, not descriptions. Here, you won't find here the dry
catalogue of wingbars and eye-rings that makes most bird books a chore. Instead, every
chapter is a story, and most of the stories turn out, in the end, to be about
us. A bird's looks and habits are part of it; how the bird gets along with
people is more of it; and somewhere in the telling you'll find you are also
learning about your own culture and what it values. You might even find out
more about yourself. 

The relationship between these birds and the people of this place has sometimes
worked beautifully. Other times it has fractured in ways that cost everyone involved.
It's not simple. That is exactly what makes it worth your attention. The birds
are not just *them*. They are *us*.

The ground covered here is deliberately small. It centers on Kaka‘ako and runs
along the coast, east to Ala Moana, and especially Ala Moana Beach Park; west
into the historic heart of downtown Honolulu, around the government and church
buildings. It's a green corridor hugging the shoreline: open lawns,
brackish canals, rocky coral edges, and a few enormous trees. That modest range
holds nearly two dozen species. That's just enough variety to handle when each 
bird has a story. Cast the net wider, over the whole island, and the stories 
would collapse into a checklist; a longer list is a thinner book. Begin here. 
Learn these birds. Get to know not just their names but their lives among us.

There is a deeper pattern running underneath, and it is worth naming before you
begin. Almost every bird in this book was brought here, much as a large proportion of everyone
who lives here came from somewhere else. They arrived by nearly every route
people use to carry an animal: for pest control and for the cooking pot, for
beauty and for song, as cage birds that escaped, and — a thousand years ago, in
the founding canoes of the first voyagers — as the moa, carried as food into an
unknown ocean. Looked at that way, the introduced birds are an immigrant
story told in feathers: each one a fragment of a homeland, brought by people
starting over while holding a small piece of where they began. But some of the
birds you see were never carried at all. The natives: the heron at the canal,
the shorebirds down each winter from the Arctic, the endangered koloa, and the
white tern that closes the book. They belong to the place itself, the older thread
beneath the immigrant one. The birds, introduced and native, carry the whole layered
history of people and this isolated land.

A word on trust. This is a small book of true things. Every claim in it that
could be checked has been; against first-hand observation, against the
published record, or both. Where something remains a guess, it's offered
as a guess. You can take the stories as accurate as I know them. But you should
still go see for yourself. That's the whole idea.

So: learn the common ones. Then look up, near the ocean, and out across the
lawn. Once you can see what is usually there, you will be ready to be surprised
by what isn't. A rare and beautiful stranger, sooner or later, will look
back at you.


# Cattle Egret

*Bubulcus ibis* · introduced to O‘ahu, 1959

A bird that was brought here to do a job, did it, and then quit to do
something far more interesting.

## A solution that became a problem

In the summer of 1959 the Territory's Board of Agriculture and Forestry,
backed by cattle ranchers and the Hui Manu Society, shipped in 105 young
cattle egrets from southern Florida. The idea was tidy: the
birds would ride with the herds, eat the flies and ticks that cost
ranchers hide quality and weight gain, and earn their keep as living
pest control. It worked. It also did not stop there. Within two
decades the statewide population had climbed past 30,000, and the bird
that had been imported as a remedy was being recategorized as a pest in
its own right.

That arc — from imported solution to naturalized problem — is the frame
for everything else about this bird in Kaka‘ako. What makes it worth a
chapter is not that the introduction went wrong, but *why* it went so
thoroughly right for the egret.

## Built to follow a mower it had never seen

The cattle egret did not evolve alongside cattle. It evolved in Africa,
following the megafauna — the buffalo, the elephants, the antelope —
walking just ahead of those great moving feet and snatching the
grasshoppers and other insects the herd flushed out of the grass.
The relationship is commensal and efficient: a cattle egret foraging
beside a large mover catches several times more prey than one hunting
alone. When tractors and mowers replaced animals on the world's
pastures, the egret made the substitution without missing a step. A
machine that stirs insects out of the grass is, to this bird, simply a
slow metal buffalo.

You can watch the whole evolutionary argument play out on a weekday
morning at Kaka‘ako Waterfront Park. When the landscape crews bring out
the mowers, a loose herd of egrets forms up behind the blades and walks
the cut line, stabbing at whatever the machine throws up. They
show no fear of the engine noise. They are not tolerating the mower;
they are *using* it, exactly as their ancestors used the buffalo.

## The opportunist's second act

The same flexibility that let the egret adopt the mower let it adopt the
prawn pond. When commercial freshwater prawn farming opened at Kahuku,
the egrets found the shallow, densely stocked ponds easier hunting than
any pasture, and poaching prawns became a recognized economic problem
for the farms almost from the day they opened. A bird imported to *help* one kind of
agriculture had, within a generation, become a cost line for another.

## What it costs the natives

The cattle egret's success is not free. It is now listed as invasive in
Hawai‘i, and beyond the prawn losses it competes with — and preys on the
nests of — native waterbirds including the koloa, the ae‘o (Hawaiian
stilt), and the ‘alae ke‘oke‘o (Hawaiian coot). An elegant white
bird on a green lawn is a pleasant thing to photograph; it is also a
reminder that "useful" and "harmless" are not the same word.

## Field notes

In breeding season the adult grows orange-buff plumes across the crown,
back, and breast, and the bill flushes orange to red; the rest of the
year it is plain white. Immature birds carry a black bill.


# Red Junglefowl

*Gallus gallus* · moa · brought by the first voyagers, roughly a thousand years ago

We all call it a chicken. The word is not wrong, but it's misleading, because
it buries the thing that matters most about this bird: of every species in
this book, the so-called chicken has the deepest roots in these islands. And
it was here the better part of a thousand years before many of the others.

## A name older than all the rest

Begin with the isolation. These islands sit farther from any continent than
almost any land on Earth. Now imagine preparing to sail toward them across open
ocean. Toward a place you only believe is out there, with no certainty of
landfall and no way back. What would you carry? Food enough for the crossing,
of course. But if you meant to stay, you would carry something more: living
animals to breed, founding stock to feed the generations that would come after
you in a homeland you had not yet laid eyes on.

That is exactly what the first discoverers of Hawai‘i did. The Polynesian
voyagers who found these islands brought, on the founding canoes, the wild red
junglefowl of Southeast Asia — the ancestor of every chicken alive — along with
the pigs and the taro and the rest of the bundle of canoe plants and animals. This
is what turns an island into a familiar home. They carried it for its meat,
its eggs, its feathers, and its place in ceremony. And they had a name
for it. They called it **moa**, and a thousand years on, people here still call it
that.

## What the word "chicken" hides

Say *chicken* and you summon a barnyard, a coop, a leftover gone wild. Say
*moa* and you summon a voyage — one of the great feats of human navigation,
crossing the largest ocean on the planet in an open canoe. This bird
is not farmyard background. It's a deliberate provision of that crossing, the
founding livestock of a founding people, carried into the unknown so that a
homeland not yet seen would have something to eat. To learn what the chicken
really is, then, is to learn something about the people who brought it. This
is the quiet argument of this whole book. The most familiar bird of all turns
out to hold the oldest human story of this place.

## The bird in its genes

Look closely and the living bird is a record with two layers. The feral
chickens crossing the lawns and parking lots today are a genetic blend: the
ancient moa underneath, overlaid with ordinary modern domestic chickens that
escaped within living memory. Many domestic chickens were set loose when Hurricanes Iwa in
1982 and Iniki in 1992 tore the coops apart across the islands. A 2022 genetic
study reads both layers in the same birds. So the rooster strutting
across the asphalt carries two histories at once in a single body: a voyage a
thousand years ago, and a single stormy night of escape. The deep past and the
recent accident, braided together in one ordinary bird.

## The first immigrants' bird

Every other introduced bird in this book is a piece of some later homeland: a
Brazilian cardinal, an African waxbill, an Asian bulbul. Each carried here in
the last two centuries by people who came holding a fragment of where they were
from. The moa is the first of them all, and the deepest. It is the bird of the
first immigrants: the people who found these islands and stayed, who carried
their food-bird across an ocean into the unknown so that life could continue on
the far side of the world. We call it a chicken and step
around it in the road. It's the oldest tie, in feathers, to the first human
story of this place.


# Rock Dove

*Columba livia* · the Pigeon · introduced to Hawai‘i, around 1796

This is the bird the book exists to test. It is easy to admire a saffron
finch; it takes a little discipline to give the pigeon the same honest
look. But a fair field guide does not grade on charm, so here is the
pigeon, looked at squarely.

## The oldest immigrant

For all the disdain it collects, the rock dove was here first. It reached
Hawai‘i around 1796 — earlier than the mynah, the egret, or the saffron
finch. Arriving not as wildlife but as livestock, the feral descendants
of domestic pigeons kept for food, for message-carrying, and for show. Its wild ancestors were cliff birds of Europe, Africa, and Asia,
nesting on rocky sea ledges and desert escarpments. That ancestry
is the key to everything people hold against it. A modern city, to a rock
dove, is not a degraded place. It's an excellent imitation of a sea
cliff: sheer faces, sheltered ledges, updrafts, and crevices for nesting.
The pigeon downtown is not slumming. It's precisely, expertly at home. The
bird most people read as a symbol of urban decay is in fact one of the
best-adapted animals in the city.

## A flock is a mirror

Watch where the pigeons gather — a bus stop, a plaza, a fast-food
forecourt — and you are really watching a map of human habit. The birds
collect where people gather because some of those people feed them, day
after day, until the spot becomes a fixture on the flock's daily round. The degradation that follows — the fouled pavement, the litter of
crumbs and droppings, the general dinginess of a heavily used corner — is
a joint production of human traffic and bird activity, and it is easy to
blame entirely on the bird. But the pigeon did not choose the bus
stop. We did, every time someone opened a bag of bread there. The flock is
less a nuisance the bird commits than a habit we taught it.

The proof is in the birds that no one is feeding. Away from the handouts,
a rock dove behaves like any other seed-eater. Watch it working quietly across a
lawn for grass seed, unhurried, not scavenging a heap of trash.
Observe those lawn birds for a while and the disfavor lifts; they are simply
doing what birds do. The trash-pile pigeon and the lawn pigeon are the same
species behaving in two settings, and it's the feeders who manufacture the
first one. And, with it, the reputation the whole species carries. The
people most responsible for the bird's bad name are precisely the ones who
think they are being kind to it.

## Feeding, and the law

Hawai‘i has tried to legislate the habit. Excessive feeding of feral birds
is a public-health nuisance under state law. This is defined, concretely, as
feeding in a way a reasonable person would expect to draw twenty-five or
more feral birds to linger within a twenty-foot radius. There is a fine for a
first offense and a petty misdemeanor for a repeat. The law exempts
permitted racing and show pigeons, the bird's respectable relations.
In practice enforcement is thin. By some accounts, no citations have been
issued at all. And the feeding goes on, with the pigeon as its usual
beneficiary. The bird breaks no law; only the feeder does,
and the feeder is rarely stopped.

## The flock that was white

The pigeon's colors are not even fixed across a lifetime of watching. In
the 1960s the flocks I knew were pure white. Every bird. And a
white flock wheeling together was something to behold. Over the
years that followed, the ordinary colors arrived, and the white gave way to
the mottled mix you see today. I was sorry to watch it happen; for a while
the early mixtures were less lovely than the white had been, and I suspect
the "motley" look the flock eventually took on has added to the species'
disfavor. Now a few interesting colors abound, though some are
easier on the eye than others.

There is real biology under that change, and it is not settled science. A
white flock points back to a founding stock of white domestic birds. As
other escapees joined and the gene pool mixed, the colors multiplied. What's striking is that the mixing did not simply wash back to
the wild ancestor. Geneticists once expected feral flocks to drift toward
the original blue-barred plumage over the generations. There is real
pressure that way, since blue-bar birds are caught by peregrine falcons
less often than pale ones. Yet city flocks stay stubbornly varied, and
exactly why is still debated. The shifting palette of a pigeon
flock is a live question in evolutionary biology, not just street scenery.

## Worth a second look

There is a reward for the discipline of looking. Because feral pigeons
descend from centuries of domestic breeds, no two birds in a flock are
quite alike. Blue-bars, checkers, rusty reds, pieds, and the descendants
of those old whites, all mixed together where a truly wild species would
look uniform. The iridescence on a gray neck, in the right light,
is as fine as anything on a more celebrated bird. Learning to see the
pigeon — really see it — is part of learning to see at all. That, after
all, is the whole point.


# House Sparrow

*Passer domesticus* · the English Sparrow · introduced to Hawai‘i, 1871

Small, brown, everywhere, and almost entirely beneath notice. Until a
crumb is at stake. The house sparrow is the bird you have seen ten
thousand times and never once looked at.

## The oldest partnership

Its very ordinariness hides the most remarkable thing about it: of all the
birds in this book, the house sparrow has the deepest history with us. Long
before it reached Hawai‘i, it had spent something like ten thousand years
following human agriculture out of the Near East. Its lineage expanded in
step with the Neolithic spread of farming, until it had gone wherever grain
and settlement went, which is very nearly everywhere. The bond isn't
habit but inheritance: the marks of human-commensalism are written into
the sparrow's genome, and its beak and skull have been reshaped over the
millennia to crack the grains that humans grow. It reached these
islands late in that long story. Nine birds shipped from New Zealand to
O‘ahu in 1871. Within a decade it had taken, in one early account, "the
chief place" in Honolulu. A bird built by farming had found another
town to live in.

## The tiny thief

You meet it properly at a picnic. Leave a plate unattended for a moment and
the brave ones drop in from a nearby perch — a railing, a hedge, a parked
car — and make off with scraps that look far too large for so small a bird
to carry. This is the sparrow's signature, and it marks the exact
difference between this bird and the pigeon, earlier in this book. No one is
feeding the sparrows. We coax the pigeon into its flocks with handfuls of
bread; the sparrow asks no permission and waits for no invitation. It simply
helps itself. The nerve is not rudeness. It's the same trait,
practiced for ten thousand years, that let a small brown bird ride human
agriculture clear around the world. The thief at your picnic table is very,
very good at its job.

## In the park

Away from the tables the sparrow is quieter company. In the parks you find
them down in the lawn in small flocks, working the grass for seed alongside
the other seed-eaters — saffron finches and the rest — part of the ordinary
foreground of any Honolulu green space. They are a piece of the
basic set: learn the sparrow in the lawn, and you have one more of the
common birds firmly in hand.

## Worth noticing

The male repays a close look. Under the dull first impression he wears a
gray cap, a black bib, and a warm chestnut sweep behind the eye. A tidy,
well-marked little bird, not drab at all once you actually attend to him. The sparrow's invisibility is a failure of our attention, not of
the bird. Notice it, and the commonest bird at your feet turns out to have
the longest story of them all.


# House Finch

*Haemorhous mexicanus* · the Papaya Bird, the Linnet · introduced to Hawai‘i, around 1870

This is the bird that can make you a birder. Not because it is rare or
dramatic. It is neither. But because telling it apart from the house
sparrow is the first real test of the eye, and passing it changes how you
see.

## Two birds, one lawn

The house finch sets a trap that the rest of this book's birds do not. Its
name echoes the house sparrow's; the two are close in size and both are
brownish; and they feed side by side on the same lawns, after the same
seeds. A beginner glances at the pair, files them both
under "little brown bird," and moves on. That glance is the thing this
chapter is here to break. Learning to split these two on sight is the exact
moment looking becomes birding. And once you can do it, you cannot go back
to not seeing it.

## Reading the marks

The males are a gift to the beginner. A male house finch is washed with red
across the face, breast, and rump — sometimes more orange or even yellow,
depending on what the bird has been eating — while the male house sparrow
wears a gray cap and a black bib at the throat. Red against black:
no real contest there.

The females are the true lesson, because both are plain brown and neither
helps you with color. Here the deciding mark is the breast. The house finch
is **streaked** below — blurry brown streaks running down a pale breast and
flanks, a textured look — while the house sparrow is **clean** below,
unstreaked and grayish, with a pale line swept back from the eye.
Streaked breast, finch; plain breast, sparrow. That one mark settles most
birds you will meet. If you want to confirm it, the finch carries a bigger,
thicker bill and tends to perch upright with the tail angled down; the
sparrow's bill is a stouter cone and its face is the more boldly patterned
of the two.

## Habitat and the papaya

The bird's other names point you to a second kind of clue. *Papaya bird*,
*manu ʻai mīkana* — the bird that eats papaya. The house finch
takes fruit and buds as happily as it takes lawn seed, and you will find it
up in the trees and at the fruit where a house sparrow, tied more closely to
buildings and human scraps, is less likely to be. Where a bird
feeds, and on what, is itself a field mark. A slower one than plumage, but
just as real.

## The field guide, and the making of a birder

This is the point where a good field guide earns its keep. Especially
one written for local birds, which mercifully shrinks the whole confusing
world down to the dozen or so species you will actually meet on a Honolulu
lawn. Work out this one pair, finch from sparrow, and something
permanent happens. You stop seeing "a little brown bird" and start seeing
two species, told apart on purpose, by marks you can name. That skill is
exactly what a birder has that a passerby does not. And it generalizes: the
next tricky pair falls to the same patience. The house finch, in other
words, is where this book's quiet promise comes true. Learn the common ones,
and the world rearranges itself into things you can tell apart.


# Red-vented Bulbul

*Pycnonotus cafer* · introduced to O‘ahu, mid-1950s

A restless dark bird with a spark of red beneath the tail, and the only
bird in this book that can be your ally and your adversary on the same
afternoon.

## A cage bird on the wrong list

Like several other birds in this book, the red-vented bulbul came in
through the cage trade. A popular pet, native to southern Asia, brought to
O‘ahu and illegally let loose in the mid-1950s. But where those
other escapes settled in quietly, this one earned a record. It feeds on
commercial fruit — papaya, mango, lychee, banana — and on flower buds. On
one year the O‘ahu orchid growers blamed it for some three hundred thousand
dollars in losses. It also spreads the seeds of serious invasive weeds like
miconia and ivy gourd. For that it sits on two lists no bird wants
to be on: the IUCN's hundred worst invasive species in the world, and the
State of Hawai‘i's Injurious Species list, which makes it illegal to release
the bird, carry it between islands, or export it without a permit.
It is established on O‘ahu and, so far, kept off the other islands. There is a danger;
it has been known to nest in a shipping container and is quite capable of
flying a channel, which is why the invasive-species committees watch the
other islands closely. The strict import rules that guard against
the next stray finch exist, in part, because of this bird.

## Never still

For all that, it's a hard bird to dislike in the moment, mostly because it
is so alive. The bulbul never seems to sit quietly; it is forever in motion
— hopping, sallying after something, flicking from branch to branch,
calling as it goes. You will know it by the dark, slightly spiky-
crested head, the scaled brown body, the white rump, and the small flash of
red under the tail that gives the bird its name.

## Friend and foe in one bird

Here is the bird's real problem, and it is an honest one: it eats both ways.
When a bulbul is working a tree for insects, it is your friend, quietly
doing the job a gardener would pay for. When the same bird takes a
single bite out of a nearly ripe papaya — and one bite is all it takes to
ruin the fruit — it's suddenly your adversary. No homeowner with a
few backyard fruit trees, and certainly no grower with a field of
susceptible ones, has much love for a fruit-eater. The
bird is not malicious; it's simply an omnivore, and omnivory makes it
gardener and thief by turns, sometimes within the same hour.

## Keeping both columns

The bulbul is the hardest bird in this book to sum up in a word, and that is
exactly why it belongs here. The fair way to hold it is to keep both columns
open — the insects it takes away on one side, the fruit it spoils on the
other — and to resist collapsing the two into a single verdict. That, in the
end, is all an honest field guide does: it tells you what the bird does, and
leaves the accounting to you. The pigeon asked you to look at a disliked bird
fairly; the bulbul asks something harder. To look at a genuinely costly bird
fairly, and to grant it its virtues without pretending away its damages. A
reader who can do that with the bulbul can do it with anything.


# Common Mynah

*Acridotheres tristis* · Piha‘e-kelo · introduced from India, 1865

The mynah is so ordinary in Honolulu that almost no one looks at it. That
is a pity, because it's one of the few birds in the city you can hold a
conversation with — and one of the few that carries a small literary
heartbreak.

## An older arrival

The mynah did not come for our pleasure. It came nearly a century before
the cattle egret, brought from India in 1865 and soon established on every
main Hawaiian island. Its Hawaiian name is
*Piha‘e-kelo*. Unlike the songbirds the Hui Manu
would later import for their color and their voices, the mynah arrived in
the practical era of introductions, to work against insect pests.
It outlasted its job and stayed, and today it walks the sidewalks of
Kaka‘ako with the proprietary stride of a bird that has been here longer
than most of the buildings.

## Talking to a mynah

For years I told my introductory biology students that they might catch me 
doing something odd as I walked across campus. If I passed a pair of mynahs 
I would stop, turn to face them, tuck my hands into my armpits, and nod 
my head three times. I was talking to them, in their own posture, 
and I was saying *this is my territory.*

Here's what I noticed, and it was a hypothesis more than a certainty: a
**visiting** pair would usually fly off, while the **resident** pair would
hold their ground and nod back. I read the fleeing birds as
strangers caught outside their own ground, and the birds that answered as
residents defending theirs. I didn't truly know the boundaries of any
mynah's territory; what I had was the difference in how the two pairs
responded to the same rude challenge. That difference was enough to
make the guess worth holding. The head-bob itself is a real part of the
mynah's repertoire. Whether the nod returned to me was strictly a
territorial answer or something nearer to alarm, I can't swear. But the
ones who stayed, answered.

How often do you get to talk to an animal and get a reply, in their language,
you can believe?

## Pairs by day, a crowd by night

By day you almost always see mynahs in twos, and they are easy to be near.
They will let you approach far closer than most wild birds bother to
allow. But the count changes at dusk. As the light goes, the
day's scattered pairs abandon their separate haunts and stream toward a
few particular big trees where they gather. The pattern
is the bird's own: territorial in pairs through the working day, then
communal by night, scores to thousands of birds to a single roost.
Stand in the right spot near dusk and you will be startled by how many
pour past overhead, all bound for the same tree.

These are mynah trees, and they announce themselves. The birds chatter at
full volume for a long while before they settle, the racket carrying well
past dark. It's a marvel to witness and a misery to live
beside. A mynah tree is emphatically not one you would want in your own
yard.

## The wrong species

People sometimes ask whether our mynah can talk. They are half-remembering
a talking mynah from a television show. They have the wrong bird. The famous
talker is the **Hill Mynah** (*Gracula religiosa*), a different genus
altogether, ranked among the finest mimics of human speech in the world,
the equal of an African grey parrot. Honolulu got *Acridotheres
tristis* instead: the bird everyone sees is not the talking species.

I met one of those talking stars once. I happened to see it on what turned
out to be the last occasion it ever spoke; it died in the night after my
visit. I have been a little sorry about the whole genus ever
since. And sorrier still since I read, as a student, Aldous Huxley's last
novel. In *Island* (1962), Huxley fills his utopia with mynahs trained to
call out two phrases across the gardens: *"Attention!"* and *"Here and now."* They are the island's reminder to stay present, to stop
sleepwalking through one's own life. I always thought it a marvelous idea,
and I was quietly disappointed that the mynah we were given is the cousin
that cannot give us these reminders.

But we do have, on our lawns, a bird you can talk to, which may also talk back.
The mynah at your feet will never tell you to pay attention. 
But stop, tuck in your hands, and nod at it three times, and it might 
might answer. That's good enough.


# Red-crested Cardinal

*Paroaria coronata* · the Brazilian Cardinal · introduced to O‘ahu, around 1928

After a run of birds you have to train your eye to notice, here is one you
cannot miss: a sharp gray-and-white bird wearing a brilliant red head, and
the only bird in the park that seems to look back at you.

## The bird that looks back

In a park like Kaka‘ako Waterfront Park, the red-crested cardinal is the
one that closes the distance. They travel almost always as a pair, and if
you stand still a pair will often work its way toward you, coming surprisingly
close before pausing a moment — heads up, eyeing you — as if taking your
measure. The field literature would call this boldness rather than
friendliness: the species is confident, thoroughly at ease around people, and
not above a little aggression. But from the standing end of the
encounter, this reads as frank curiosity, and it makes this the most companionable
bird in the park.

## Brought for its color

The cardinal is here for exactly the reason it catches your eye. A native of
south-central South America — Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, southern Brazil —
it was introduced to O‘ahu around 1928 to 1931, primarily by the **Hui Manu
Society**, the group of Honolulu bird enthusiasts who imported songbirds for
their beauty and their voices. Where the mynah came for pest control
and the sparrow as an accidental world-traveler, this bird was brought on
purpose, for its looks. (It is not, despite the name, a true cardinal at all,
but a tanager. The resemblance is borrowed.)

And the looks were the point in a way that goes deeper than ornament. The
seed-eaters already on these lawns were browns and yellows and grays; the
red-crested cardinal arrived carrying a bright pure red and a clean white
breast — a color the local flock simply had not had. The Hui Manu
imported it for that red eighty years before I noticed the same red filling
the same gap; we arrived at the bird's value from opposite ends of a century.

## Watching it arrive

I can place my own first sight of one exactly. It was beside the Waikiki
Aquarium, and it stopped me. I knew the urban birds well by then, and here
was one that did not fit any of them. Too vivid, too unfamiliar. It
was the second bird I have watched move into Honolulu (the saffron finch is
the other), and the cardinal's spread was its own slow business: established
first on O‘ahu and Kaua‘i, then working outward island by island over the
following decades. The startle of that first one by the aquarium is
the feeling the whole book is about. The new bird that breaks the pattern
you have learned, and announces itself.

## Impossible to miss

The red-crested cardinal is the opposite of the sparrow. You do not have to
train your eye to find it; it finds you, and its color does the rest. For many
people it is the gateway bird. The first introduced species they ever notice
on purpose. A pair moving across a lawn, sometimes loosely mixed
with the other seed-eaters but most often just the two of them, is one of the
easiest and most rewarding sights to add to your list. Once you have
it, you will never again not see it.


# White-rumped Shama

*Copsychus malabaricus* · introduced to O‘ahu, 1940

This is the hardest bird in the book to lay eyes on, and quite possibly the
finest voice in it. You will almost certainly hear it long before you see it —
if you see it at all.

## Heard before seen

The shama is uncommon in our stretch of town, and for a specific reason: it
wants dense shade and thick understory, the deep green tangle of a forest
floor. The open coastal corridor of lawns and canals offers very little of
that. You find it only where a real pocket of shade has been allowed
to survive. Mine is a huge banyan not far from the King Kamehameha statue,
where I have come upon one on several separate visits. But do not be
surprised to find it absent when you go looking. It's not a common bird here,
and it keeps its own counsel. The likeliest first sign of it is not a
shape at all but a sound: out of the shade, unexpectedly, a voice.

## The voice

And what a voice. The white-rumped shama is famous across Asia as one of the
great singers of the bird world, and a moment's listening tells you why.
The song is a varied run of loud, clear, flutelike whistles. Never a simple
phrase merely repeated, but a shifting, inventive line that keeps moving, often
stitched through with flawless mimicry of other birds. Its a song that surprises you
even as it delights you. Loud, clear, unexpected. It carries
for hundreds of meters, and in the hush of a downtown courtyard, under a great
tree, it stops you where you stand.

## Brought for that song

The shama did not wander here. It was brought to O‘ahu in 1940 by the Hui Manu
Society, the very same group that gave Honolulu the red-crested cardinal a
decade or so before. But the two birds answer two different halves of
that society's purpose. The Hui Manu set out to import *colorful and melodious*
songbirds, and where the cardinal was chosen for its color, the shama was
chosen for its voice. Hold the two together and you can read the
society's whole intention in a pair of living birds: one for the eye, one for
the ear. Of all the birds in these pages, the shama is the one that was carried across
an ocean for its voice alone.

## The curious one

There is one more thing I carried away from those meetings under the banyan. The
shama struck me as a genuinely curious bird: when I finally found it and stood
still to watch, it seemed every bit as interested in looking at me as I was in
looking at it. Rare, half-hidden, kept
to the few corners where the city still holds a little deep shade, it announces
itself by the very voice it was carried here to provide. And then, when at last
you track that voice into the green and find its singer, it turns and meets your
eye with what looks, unmistakably, like your own curiosity reflected back. Of
all the birds in this book, the shama may be the one that most rewards the thing
the whole book has been asking of you: learn to listen, follow the sound into
the shade, and a rare and beautiful stranger will look back at you.


# Saffron Finch

*Sicalis flaveola* · introduced from South America, around 1965

If the mynah and the egret are old residents, the saffron finch is the
new neighbor. A small, vivid yellow bird whose arrival in Honolulu is
recent enough that you could have watched it happen.

## The bird that let itself in

The first two birds in this book came by deliberate public programs. The
mynah was brought in 1865 to fight insect pests; the cattle egret in 1959
as living fly control. The saffron finch came by a third route — the
private cage-bird trade. It is a popular aviary bird in its native South
America, kept for its color and its song, and it established itself in
Hawai‘i around 1965 from cage birds that escaped or were deliberately
turned loose.

Much of that release can be traced to a single place. The ranchland at Pu‘u Wa‘a
Wa‘a on the Big Island was, through the mid-1900s, a kind of ground zero
for setting exotic finches and waxbills free. Lavender waxbills,
cordon-bleus, seedeaters, weavers; released birds by the cageful. Most of those species flared and vanished. The saffron finch is
one of the few that caught. So the natural question — why was a tropical
South American finch sitting in a cage on a Hawaiian ranch at all? — has a
tidy answer: an era of amateur aviculture put it there, and the open
island did the rest. The casual bird-keeping that seeded it is now the
very thing the state regulates against; importing a pet bird to Hawai‘i
today takes permits and quarantine, precisely because of stories like this
one.

## A colonization you can watch

On the Big Island the saffron finch settled in quickly and is now common
across the lowlands. O‘ahu has been slower, and that slowness is
what makes the bird interesting: its spread here has unfolded within
living memory. Birds turned up sporadically around Honolulu in the 1990s;
through the 2000s they appeared in Waipahu, Mililani, ‘Ewa Beach, and
Nānākuli; by the early 2010s they had reached Nu‘uanu Valley and the
North Shore toward Hale‘iwa. Today they are locally common on
O‘ahu, favoring exactly the kind of open, short-grass coastal ground that
Kaka‘ako's lawns provide.

I can date the bird's absence precisely. In 2002 I drew up a list of birds
for University of Hawai‘i students to find as a laboratory exercise, and I
was watching the urban birds closely in those years — campus and city
both. The saffron finch was not on the list, because it was not yet here to
see; had it been around, I would have found it. A few years later
I did spot one, but it was still too scarce to photograph. I have been on
the hunt ever since, and the hunt has gotten easier: now I can count on
seeing them at Kaka‘ako Waterfront Park, and sometimes at Ala Moana Beach
Park. It's not the only bird I have watched cross from
first-sighting to commonplace. But it is one of the most recent, and one
of the most satisfying to have caught in the act. Most introduced birds in
this book are finished stories; the saffron finch is one whose map is still
being drawn.

## Knowing one when you see it

The male is the easy one: bright yellow with a distinct orange wash across
the crown that separates him from the other yellowish finches around
town. Females and males look broadly alike, though females are
duller and more olive-brown, and juveniles are buffier still — gray-brown
with only a hint of yellow. On a green Kaka‘ako lawn the adult
male is hard to miss; the rest of the family takes a second look.


# Common Waxbill

*Estrilda astrild* · introduced to O‘ahu, 1970s

A bird that's small enough to overlook entirely — until you catch the red mask
across its eyes, worn like a cartoon bandit's bandana. After that you will
never mistake it for anything else.

## A latecomer from Africa

The common waxbill is one of the newest arrivals in this book, and one I
watched turn up in person. A small finch of sub-Saharan Africa and a long-
popular cage bird, it established itself on O‘ahu in the 1970s from escaped
cage birds. A flock was first noted in a cane field near ‘Ewa Beach in 1973 
and within about fifteen years it had spread through most of the island's
lowlands. It's the second bird in these pages to arrive that way,
by the same back door as the saffron finch: a cage bird that let itself out.
For me it simply appeared one day in the places I visit, the third species I
have watched move into Honolulu. It's so tiny that I may well have
walked past it many times before I ever registered it.

## The bandit at the grass stalk

The mask is the thing to learn: a bright red stripe straight through the eye,
exactly a small thief's bandana, on an otherwise finely barred gray-brown
bird. Once you see this, the bird is unmistakable. Watch a waxbill
feed and you get a small lesson in mechanics: it will fly up and land partway
along a tall seed stalk, and its slight weight is just enough to bow the stalk
gently over, lowering the ripe seedhead to where the bird can strip it.
The bird is its own tool for bending the harvest down to itself.

## A flock that leapfrogs

Waxbills go about in flocks, sometimes large ones, and a feeding flock moves
in a way worth stopping for. Rather than walking steadily, the whole group
travels in little hops. The birds at the back flitter over the ones in front
and drop down ahead of them, so the flock rolls forward across a lawn in a
continuous leapfrog. They are wary, too, and here they part company
with the bold red-crested cardinal: a waxbill will not let you approach. Press
toward them and the flock lifts as one to a nearby tree and waits you out.

## Not the most common bird in the world

There is a thing often said about this bird — I had read it myself — that the
common waxbill is the most common bird species on Earth. It is not. That
distinction belongs to another small African seed-eater it's easy to confuse
it with, the red-billed quelea. That bird has a breeding population of something
like a billion and a half. But the waxbill is one of the most widely
*introduced* finches. Why? It was carried around the world by the cage trade.
(The widest-traveled of all, as it happens, is the house sparrow, met
earlier.)

The correction is worth making because it lays a small worry to rest. Believing
I was watching the world's commonest bird, I expected, after a few years, to
find waxbills absolutely everywhere. And when a park visit turned up none, I
began to fear they had gone. They had not. A locally common, patchy introduced
finch is precisely what the waxbill is: here on this lawn, absent from that
one, never promised at any particular place. They are still
about, and the pleasure of them is exactly that they are not guaranteed.


# Java Sparrow

*Lonchura oryzivora* · the Java rice bird · introduced to O‘ahu, 1960s

In the brown-and-streaky crowd of small seed-eaters, the Java sparrow is the
one in formal wear. It's the best-dressed bird on the lawn, and the only one
you can never mistake for anything else.

## Unmistakable

The markings settle the identification in a glance: a clean pale-gray body, a
black cap and tail, a crisp white cheek, a bold red ring around the eye, and a
heavy pink-red bill set in the black of the face. Set that
beside the muddled browns of the sparrows and finches and waxbills it feeds
among, and there is simply no confusing it. The Java sparrow is tailored,
formal, almost dapper. If you scored these birds on looks, this one would
finish near the top. And that stout red bill is a plain declaration of
the bird's business: it is a serious seed-cracker. Its scientific name,
*oryzivora*, means "rice-eater," and across much of the world it is known
simply as the rice bird.

## A flock at the uncut lawn

You meet it in flocks, and in a particular sort of spot: a patch the mowing
crew has missed, where the grass has been left long enough to go to seed. That is where the food is — heads of grass heavy with seed — and that
is where the Java sparrows gather to work it over. It is a bird that profits
from a neglected corner; a missed mowing is its banquet.

## Brought for its beauty

So why is it here at all? For the reason you would guess from looking at it. The
Java sparrow is one of the most popular cage birds on Earth. Its been kept for centuries
for its handsome plumage and its gentle, calm manner. It reached O‘ahu in the
1960s the same way the saffron finch and the waxbill did. It came through the cage
trade, as birds that escaped or were released. Like the red-crested
cardinal, it's here because someone found it beautiful. The same aesthetic
impulse that the Hui Manu Society formalized when it imported songbirds for
their color worked again, less formally, with this one. Beauty was its passport.

## The cost of being beautiful

And that beauty has been a disaster at home. In its native Java and Bali, where
it was once abundant in the rice fields, the Java sparrow has been trapped so
relentlessly for the cage trade — by one survey, more than a third of the
adults taken in a single year — that it is now listed as Endangered. There are fewer
than ten thousand wild birds left and the number still falling. Hold
that fact against the lawn in front of you, where the same species feeds in an
easy, untroubled flock. It's the mirror image of the koloa, the endemic duck of these
islands. The koloa is vanishing at home, here in Hawai‘i; the Java
sparrow is a bird vanishing at *its* home, and flourishing here as an exile.
Both losses run through the same human hand. The one careless, the other
covetous. The Java sparrow carries the sharper paradox of the two: it's
being trapped toward extinction in Java for precisely the beauty that has made
it secure on a patch of grass five thousand miles away. The handsomest bird in
the lawn is a refugee from its own desirability.


# Zebra Dove

*Geopelia striata* · introduced to O‘ahu, 1922

The small barred dove at your feet is the most easily overlooked bird in
Honolulu, for the simple reason that it is everywhere. It has become part of
the pavement. And, like the pavement, it goes unseen.

## Underfoot everywhere

The zebra dove is the most widespread dove on the islands, and it earned that
rank quickly. Introduced in 1922 — released on O‘ahu, Moloka‘i, and Maui,
reportedly through a Singapore bird dealer — it had spread to nearly all the
main islands within about fifteen years. Today, it occupies every
developed lowland there is. You see it on lawns, but just as readily
on sidewalks, on roadsides, and out in the open of a parking lot. Wherever
there is bare ground and a fallen seed. Its very abundance is what
makes it invisible: a bird this common stops registering as a bird at all,
which is precisely the habit this book is trying to break.

## The brave little forager

Watch one and it earns back the attention. The zebra dove is a ground bird,
moving with a quick, deliberate, almost rodent-like scurry, head down, wholly
intent on finding the next seed or small insect. It is brave
out of all proportion to its size. It will work a parking lot among shoes and
tires, letting people pass close without flushing. At a good feeding
patch, the birds gather in numbers, more sociably than the larger spotted dove
of the next chapter. And for all the bird's general mildness, the
males have another side: the cooing, bowing male is the territorial one, and
he will turn aggressive in a dispute over ground or a mate.

## A singer in disguise

Here is the thing the background bird never tells you. In its Southeast Asian
homeland the zebra dove is not overlooked at all. It's a celebrated
songster, the *merbuk*, kept as a prized cage bird and entered in cooing
competitions judged on the beauty of its voice. In Honolulu it asks
for no notice whatsoever, but listen for it: that soft, rhythmic coo running
quietly under the day is this very bird. It does not call attention to itself;
it simply calls, low and steady. And an entire tradition, an ocean away, is
built on that sound. The most unremarkable bird in the park is a famous singer
who has chosen to keep quiet about it.

## Telling the two doves apart

This is the smaller of Honolulu's two everyday doves, and learning to name it
is half of an easy pair. The zebra dove is slim and long-tailed, finely barred
in black across the neck and the sides of the breast — the "zebra" that names
it — with a soft bluish-gray face. The bird in the next chapter, the
spotted dove, is distinctly bigger and wears a spotted collar instead of bars.
Bars or spots, small or large: split those two the way you learned to split
the finch from the sparrow, and one more corner of the lawn resolves back into
species.


# Spotted Dove

*Spilopelia chinensis* · the Lace-necked Dove · introduced to Hawai‘i, before 1855

The larger of Honolulu's two everyday doves keeps mostly to itself. And
wears, around its neck, the single most beautiful piece of detail in this
whole book, which almost no one has ever actually seen.

## An old arrival, brought for the pot

The spotted dove has been here a very long time. It reached the islands
before 1855. It came most likely as captive birds raised for food by Chinese
immigrants. That makes it one of the oldest birds in these pages; only the
pigeon got here ahead of it, and it predates even the mynah. A native
of southern Asia in its Chinese form, it spread quickly and is now common on
every main island. Its names carry its whole story: the *Chinese
dove* for where it came from, the *lace-necked dove* for the thing it wears.
And its way in was its own. Not pest control, not the cage-fancy trade, but
the cooking pot.

## The loner that keeps company

Where the zebra dove of the last chapter gathers in busy little crowds, the
spotted dove is the bigger, quieter bird that you usually meet alone or in a
single pair. You will know it at once by the contrast:
larger than the zebra dove, browner, and wearing across the back of the neck a
broad black patch spangled with white spots instead of the zebra's fine bars.
Spots, not bars; large, not small. The pair is easy once you have both. And
yet, now and then, this loner does something unexpected: it will settle in
among a group of the smaller seed-eaters and simply stand there with them, a
calm, larger presence at the edge of the flock, almost as if it had appointed
itself their guard.

## The spots you cannot see

The neck patch is the thing to stay with. From a few feet away the "lace"
looks like a simple scatter of pale dots on black — pretty, but plain. Put a
close-up lens on it, though, and each single spot resolves into something
intricately built, a small structured architecture of feather far more complex
than the unaided eye ever reports. I have these in rare photographs,
and they still surprise me. You do not see this standing in the park; only the
camera sees it. And that is the quiet lesson of the spotted dove: there is an
enormous amount of detail on a bird that a person simply cannot resolve.

## What the bird sees

Which raises the real question. If we cannot see it, who is it for? Not us.
Birds are built to see far more than we do. They are tetrachromats, carrying a
fourth color channel that reaches into the ultraviolet, with oil-droplet
filters in the eye that split apart colors that we read as a single shade. 
Markings
that are faint or invisible to a person can be vivid and exact to a bird. And
they are used in earnest; to tell one species from another, and one mate from
the next. The spotted dove's necklace, which we need a macro lens
even to glimpse, is plain speech to another dove. Much of this book has been an argument for
learning to see the common birds around you. Here the lesson turns around:
even once you have learned to see them, they see a far richer world than you
ever will, and there is always more on a bird than the eye is given to find.


# Black-crowned Night Heron

*Nycticorax nycticorax* · ‘auku‘u · native

You might assume a fish-eater would head for the ocean to feed. Some do;  a
white tern will fly miles out to sea for its meals. The ‘auku‘u found a smarter,
lazier table much closer to home: a canal.

## The urban café

Walk the length of Ala Moana Beach Park, back parallel to Ala Moana Boulevard, and
you'll find a canal of brackish water that rises and falls with the tide. At times it runs a foot or so deep; at low tide it draws down, and in
the depressions along its course the water is left behind as shallow ponds. Any
fish that failed to read the falling tide is now trapped in one of those ponds,
with nowhere to go. This is the night heron's opportunity, and it
works it patiently. The bird perches on the canal's edge and waits; there are
plenty of fish here, so it can afford to. The tide does the herding;
the heron only has to keep its table.

## A patient ambush

Patience is the whole of the heron's technique. It is a "stand and wait"
hunter. It settles into a hunched, motionless crouch at the water's edge,
stares down into the shallows, and holds utterly still until a fish drifts into
range, then unfolds in a single lunging strike. It's stocky and
short-necked for a heron, with a black crown and back, gray wings, pale
underparts, and a startling red eye. The name in English comes with a harsh,
carrying *quok* of a call. The "night" in the name only half applies
in Hawai‘i: mainland night herons feed mostly after dark, but the birds here
hunt by day as readily as at dusk. That's why you can watch one work the
canal in full afternoon sun.

## Tables along the water

Watch the whole length of the canal and a pattern appears. The herons are
spaced out along it, each to its own stretch, politely separated down the line
of water. They are territorial feeders, holding exclusive patches. 
But the spacing here is relaxed rather than fierce, because there is enough for
everyone; the canal is long and the fish are many. There is
something about the formality of the place. This is a straight, uniform, engineered
channel. That makes me think of how a city lays out a row of cafés: inside, diners
are seated at intervals along a counter, each working its own plate, no need to
quarrel when the food keeps coming. This is the ‘auku‘u's urban café, and the
herons are its regular patrons, each at a favorite table.

## A native at the urban table

Here is the quiet turn. For all that it dines on is in a human-built channel, the
‘auku‘u is **native**. This is Hawai‘i's only native heron, indigenous to these
islands and present here long before there was a canal, a boulevard, or a park
to name it after. And yet it has taken to this concrete, tide-fed
ditch as fluently as any introduced bird ever took to a lawn or a bus stop. It
is a useful reminder that being native is not the same as keeping apart from
us. This native fisher has simply read the city's plumbing as one more kind of
wetland, and set up its table where the water and the fish reliably meet. It
belongs here in the oldest sense, and it has learned the new Honolulu as
quickly as any newcomer.


# Ruddy Turnstone

*Arenaria interpres* · ‘akekeke · a native migratory visitor

On one of the busiest strip of sand in Honolulu, among the towels and the sunscreen,
a few small calico birds work the rocky edges almost unnoticed. They are the
easiest birds in this book to walk right past. And, mile for mile, the
greatest travelers in it.

## Hiding on a crowded beach

You have to be paying attention to see them at all. I have only ever found a
small group — perhaps five birds at a time — and in the most unlikely setting:
the heavily used sand of Ala Moana Beach Park. There, among the rocky
coral fragments at the water's edge, they poke and probe for aquatic food,
always on the move. Now and then they retreat up onto the open sand, or even
in among the concrete and lawns of the human shoreline. But make no
mistake, these are true shoreline birds. Their dull, mottled winter plumage
is a kind of calico camouflage that breaks them up against the coral and lets
them vanish in plain sight. Lie out for a tan if you like; just keep
one eye open, and you may be lucky enough to catch these elusive little
shoreline critters at work.

## The bird that turns the stones

Watch one feed and its name explains itself. The turnstone makes its living by
overturning the shoreline. It uses its strong neck and a short, slightly wedge-
shaped bill to flip aside stones, shells, and clumps of seaweed and snatch the
small invertebrates hiding underneath. That habit is the English name
outright: *turnstone*. The Hawaiian name comes from another direction
entirely — *‘akekeke*, after the bird's dry rattling call, *a-ke-ke-ke*. Special slightly spiny feet help it grip the wet rock while it works.
It's a small bird that earns its keep by lifting the lids off the edge of the
sea.

## A traveler in disguise

Here is what the sunbathers stepping around it never suspect. This
unremarkable beach bird is a native. But a native that is only visiting, and
that arrives under its own astonishing power. The ruddy turnstone is one of the
most northerly-nesting birds on Earth: it breeds on high-Arctic tundra and
crosses thousands of miles of open ocean to winter on these shores. It's here from
about August until it leaves for the Arctic again in spring. Some make
the run from Alaska to Hawai‘i in long overwater hauls of more than a thousand
kilometers a day. And the most staggering part is how the young ones
arrive. A juvenile turnstone takes its first flight at under three weeks old. 
And within a few days, it launches out over the open Pacific toward wintering
grounds it has never seen. Its parents have already gone on ahead, leaving it to
navigate thousands of miles entirely alone. The little birds among the
coral at Ala Moana did exactly that to reach this beach.

## A different kind of native

Set this bird against the rest of the book and the contrast is the whole point.
No one carried the turnstone here. It was not brought for food or song or color
or by accident; it comes on its own wings, as its ancestors have come far
longer than there have been people on these shores. This is an annual thread stitched
between the top of the world and these islands, older than the first canoe. It is native not by staying but by returning. Look down, next time, at
that crowded ribbon of sand: the calico bird flipping a chip of coral is a
traveler from the Arctic who found this exact beach without a map, and will
find the tundra again come spring.


# Pacific Golden-Plover

*Pluvialis fulva* · kōlea · a native migratory visitor

The turnstone of the last chapter roves the shoreline in a restless little
band. The kōlea is its opposite in nearly every way: it claims one patch of
open lawn, holds it alone, and rules it. The same patch, year after year. Of
all the birds in this book, it may be the one that has been in the longest and
deepest conversation with the people who live here.

## King of the lawn

Watch a kōlea and you watch a monarch survey its grounds. It stands tall and
often nearly motionless, scanning the grass; then it dashes a few steps, stabs
down for an insect working through the turf, and resumes its still, upright
watch. This is not idle loitering. It's a feeding territory, a
patch of a fraction of an acre that the bird defends through the whole winter,
August to April. I like to call them **lawn sharks**, and the name fits:
a top predator cruising its own stretch of green, picking off the unwary, a
functional echo of the reef shark on its patch of coral. And the
hunting must pay, because the bird comes back to the very same lawn the next
year, and the year after that.

## The bird that comes home

That return is the kōlea's most astonishing trait. It comes back not merely to
Hawai‘i but to the *same lawn*, with about eighty percent fidelity. One famous
banded bird returned to the same spot at Bellows, on windward O‘ahu, for
twenty-one years running. Between visits it flies to Alaska and back. That's
a nonstop run of more than three thousand miles. It takes three or four days
traveling over open ocean. It does this twice a year. 
This faithfulness is why the kōlea, almost alone
among the birds here, becomes a person's friend. Most birds are just "birds."
But a kōlea that claims your yard each August, that you watch grow fat and molt
into its black-and-gold finery and depart each spring, and that you then see
land on the same grass the following fall — that bird is given a name. People grow
genuinely attached to their annual visitor.

## Fattens, and leaves

And yet the kōlea's relationship with Hawai‘i's host culture is famously mixed,
and the reason is the very leaving that the rest of us find so poignant. There
is an ʻōlelo noʻeau — a traditional saying — *"ʻAi nō ke kōlea a momona a hoʻi
i Kahiki"*: the kōlea eats until it is fat, then returns to Kahiki, the distant
land. This is what is said of a person who comes, takes what the land freely
offers, grows prosperous on it, and then departs without giving back. The
bird's own name carries the sense of one who takes and leaves. So the
same bird that one household names and loves, the tradition casts as the
freeloader who fattens and goes. Friend and opportunist, welcomed and
side-eyed at once. It's exactly the kind of knotted, two-sided relationship
between birds and people that this book keeps turning up. The kōlea does not
let you settle on a single feeling about it.

## The birds that showed the way

There is a deeper side to the kōlea's leaving, though, and it reaches back to
the very first chapter of human life on these islands. Each spring the flocks
gather and lift off to the north. They go the same direction, on the same schedule,
every year. To a people who read the sea and sky for meaning, that's not loss;
that's information. The seasonal flights of migratory birds were among the signs
Polynesian navigators used in wayfinding, and some scholars believe the first
voyagers may have found Hawai‘i by following the spring migration of birds
northward from the islands of the south. Picture it from a canoe:
flocks streaming off each year toward an empty horizon, always the same way. A
living highway pointing toward land that no one had yet seen. Recall the moa, from near the start of this book, riding in the founding
canoe as food for an unknown homeland. The kōlea may have been the other half of that voyage: not the
provision carried aboard, but the sign in the sky that told the canoe where to
steer.

## Native by return

Like the turnstone, the kōlea is native not by staying but by returning, an
annual thread stitched between the Arctic and these islands. But where the
turnstone is hidden in a flock at the water's edge, the kōlea stands alone and in
the open and makes itself known. It claims its ground, earns a name, carries a
proverb, and may once have drawn the first people across the sea to this place. Look for the still bird standing sentinel on the grass, watching
its domain. It will be back next year, on that same spot. It always is.


# Hawaiian Duck

*Anas wyvilliana* · koloa maoli · endemic; endangered

Linger at the canal long enough and a duck or two will paddle past. A duck —
and if you know your mainland birds, you think *mallard*, and you look away.
That glance, it turns out, is the whole tragedy of this bird in miniature.

## A duck that isn't a mallard

It does look like a mallard. Specifically like a female mallard, mottled brown
all over. But this is the koloa, Hawai‘i's own duck, an endemic species found
nowhere else on Earth. A pure koloa is darker than a mallard and a
fifth to a third smaller, and its full Hawaiian name, *koloa maoli*, means the
"true" or "native" duck. It's a name that, as we will see, is doing painful work. It is federally endangered, and it is hiding, in plain sight, behind
the most familiar duck-face in the world.

## Nowhere much to be a duck

There is not a lot of room to be a duck in this part of town. The koloa is not
an ocean bird; you will not find it out in the surf. What it needs is
fresh, calm water, and in the Kaka‘ako region that comes only in a few mostly
man-made pockets: the pond that wraps the State Capitol has drawn ducks, and
there are a few spots down by the Zoo, but it's the canal in our stretch that
is the real magnet. This is not a big area, but it's home to a few
birds. An endemic duck of the most isolated archipelago on Earth,
reduced in the city to a handful of engineered ponds and a tidal ditch that is
only sometimes fresh enough to suit it.

## Two threats, one of them quiet

The shortage of habitat is one pressure on the koloa. There are others: 
introduced cats, rats, and mongooses take eggs and ducklings; and people have
always had a use for a duck. Nearly every major cuisine makes a place for one. But the gravest threat is the one almost no one would guess,
and it is genetic. The koloa freely interbreeds with feral mallards, and the
hybrids are steadily swallowing the native gene pool. This is a slow genetic dilution
that biologists describe, without exaggeration, as genetic extinction.
On O‘ahu it is already far advanced: most of the island's koloa-looking ducks
are now koloa–mallard hybrids, and genetically pure koloa survive mainly on
Kaua‘i.

## The cruelest resemblance

So return to that first careless glance. The very likeness that lets a visitor
file the koloa away as a mallard is the same likeness that lets the two birds
interbreed. The koloa is being lost not by being shot or starved but by being
*blended away*. Each generation becomes a little more mallard, a little less koloa,
until the endemic persists only as a fraction inside a mixed bird.
There is no villain in this, which is what makes it the hardest kind of loss to
tell: someone, somewhere, let a few mallards loose on ordinary-looking
water. And a bird found nowhere else on Earth began dissolving into its own
look-alike. Conservation managers fight it the only way they can. They remove
feral mallards and hybrids, guard the pure birds, and protect what wetland is
left. But it is slow, sorrowful work. Look again, then, and look
closely, at the pair paddling the canal. If they are still koloa — truly koloa
— they are among the rarest birds in this entire book, and they are watching
their own kind vanish into a resemblance.


# White Tern

*Gygis alba* · manu-o-Kū · Honolulu's official bird

The last chapters have belonged to the birds that were here before us; the
heron at the canal, the travelers down from the Arctic, the endangered koloa.
The book ends with one more native, and the most exalted of them: a bird that
belongs not to the ground or the shoreline but to the open air. And it's the bird that an
entire city has taken as its own.

## The native at the city's heart

Manu-o-Kū is native, like the others in this closing run. A seabird of the
tropical Pacific that needed no ship, no cage, no dealer, no acclimatization
society to reach these islands. But it's the native that has come closest to
us. In the main Hawaiian Islands it nests only on O‘ahu, and only in Honolulu —
in the heart of the city, over its streets and parks. 

In 2007, Honolulu
adopted it as its official bird. After a long gallery of newcomers,
and a handful of natives keeping to the canal and the shore, the book closes on
the one the city took into its very center.

(A small matter of names, settled at last. The historical Hui Manu Society
that brought so many of this book's birds for their color and their song is
*not* the modern Hui Manu-o-Kū — the volunteers who watch over this native
tern. The two names finally meet here, on the bird the second group is named
for.)

## A master of flight

Look up when you are near the ocean. You will catch two streaks of white in
perfect tandem, darting and banking, climbing to a great height and then, just
as suddenly, dashing back down — aerial acrobatics at its best. This
is a bird built wholly for air, in a way that none of the walkers and
scratchers in the earlier chapters are. It feeds far offshore, ranging
sometimes a hundred miles out for small fish and squid that larger hunters
drive to the surface. If you are lucky, you'll see one flying home with a
beakful of little fish carried crosswise, their silver bodies sticking out on
both sides of that striking black bill.

## A nest that is no nest

Its breeding habit is almost unbelievable the first time you hear it: the
white tern builds no nest at all. It lays a single egg directly onto a bare
branch — wedged into a fork, or balanced in the shallow cup left by the scar
of a trimmed limb — with nothing beneath it but air and the parents' care. Its life in the city is recent and entirely its own doing. The first
pair recorded nesting in Honolulu turned up in a tree near Hanauma Bay in
1961; from that single beginning the population has grown into the thousands. A well-kept street tree, as it happens, makes ideal habitat. So the
city's own trees have quietly become the nurseries of a seabird.

## The bird we protect

Here is the inversion the whole book has been walking toward. The white tern is
the bird this city has taken into its daily care. It has been covered
by the Migratory Bird Treaty Act since 1918; the State lists the O‘ahu
population as threatened; and the volunteers of Hui Manu-o-Kū tie informative ribbons
around any tree holding an egg or a chick, so the tree-trimmers will know to
keep clear. Consider the shape of that. We carried most of the birds in
this book across oceans — the pigeon, the sparrow, the bulbul — and then spent a
century learning to tolerate, manage, or resent them; a few others, the natives,
came on their own and kept to the edges. But this one came on its own wings into
the very middle of the city, and ordinary people tie ribbons in the street trees
to keep it safe. The book ends not with a newcomer but with the native the city
looks up to find. The white bird overhead that all of Honolulu has quietly
agreed to look after. Look up, near the ocean. It is the easiest bird in this
book to love, and the truest sign that a city built by newcomers can still
belong, in the end, to a bird that was always here.
